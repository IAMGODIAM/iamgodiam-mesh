What happened on the journey back from Rome?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 133 — The Return from Rome · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 133 tells the journey home. Jesus left Rome as quietly as he had come — the scribe of Damascus appeared without announcement and disappeared the same way — while the small groups he had taught there kept meeting in his memory. Gonod and Ganid sent their belongings ahead by pack train to Tarentum, and the three walked the Appian Way across Italy, then sailed by way of Nicopolis to Corinth, and on through Athens, Ephesus, Cyprus, and Antioch toward the desert caravan home.

“As they went on board the boat, they looked back upon the scene of the teary-eyed couple standing in silent embrace. Having heard the latter half of Jesus’ message to the man, Gonod was all day occupied with meditations thereon, and he resolved to reorganize his home when he returned to India.”

“While Ganid studied family life, Jesus was teaching Crispus the better ways of religious living. Jesus held more than twenty sessions with this forward-looking Jew; and it is not surprising, years afterward, when Paul was preaching in this very synagogue, and when the Jews had rejected his message and had voted to forbid his further preaching in the synagogue, and when he then went to the gentiles, that Crispus with his entire family embraced the new religion, and that he became one of the chief supports of the Christian church which Paul subsequently organized at Corinth.”

What this paper teaches

The road itself is the classroom. Near Tarentum an incident by the roadside detained them; at a ship landing they watched a man mistreating his wife, and after hearing Jesus speak to the man, Gonod resolved to reorganize his own home when he reached India. At Corinth, Ganid's growing interest in the Jewish religion led them to the synagogue, where Jesus held more than twenty sessions with Crispus, whose whole family later embraced the new faith when Paul preached there. The paper then follows the travelers through Athens, Ephesus, Cyprus, and Antioch before the caravan crossing home.

Why it matters

Two scenes carry this paper's weight for a liberation reading. In the first, a husband's violence against his wife is not passed by as a private matter; Jesus intervenes, and the watching merchant goes home determined to reorganize his own household — teaching that travels is teaching that changes homes. In the second, the fruit of quiet instruction ripens years later in Corinth, when people Jesus taught privately become the backbone of a church Paul organizes. The paper honors seed-work: most of what frees a people is planted without witnesses and harvested by others.
