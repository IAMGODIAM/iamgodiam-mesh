Why did Jesus withdraw to Mount Gilboa with his apostles?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 144 — At Gilboa and in the Decapolis · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 144 covers two quiet months on Mount Gilboa, where Jesus withdraws with his apostles while Jerusalem's rulers grow hostile and Herod still holds John in prison. It is a season of preparation: Jesus rehearses the twelve, tells them of his early life and the forty days, and teaches them at length about prayer — its forms, its meaning, and his own seasons of communion with the Father. The party then works quietly through the Decapolis cities until news comes that John has been executed.

“Jesus knew that the days of the preliminary work of teaching and preaching were about over, that the next move involved the beginning of the full and final effort of his life on earth, and he did not wish the launching of this undertaking to be in any manner either trying or embarrassing to John the Baptist. Jesus had therefore decided to spend some time in retirement rehearsing his apostles and then to do some quiet work in the cities of the Decapolis until John should be either executed or released to join them in a united effort.”

What this paper teaches

Three causes drove the retirement: the Jerusalem rulers were antagonistic, Herod held John in prison suspecting some association with Jesus, and tension grew between John's disciples and Jesus' apostles. Unwilling to launch the final effort in any way that embarrassed John, Jesus rehearsed the twelve at Gilboa while quiet work proceeded in the Decapolis. There Jesus disclosed much of his early life and the forty days after his baptism, charging the apostles to tell no man until after his return to the Father. Extended teaching on prayer follows — John's simple prayer, the requested model prayer, and days of questions. In November and December the twenty-four worked Scythopolis, Gerasa, Abila, and Gadara before returning near Pella, where they learned of John's execution on January 10.

Why it matters

Retreat here is strategy, not surrender: a leader refuses to build his moment on a prisoner's embarrassment, and a movement under threat uses the hills to train rather than scatter. The paper's prayer teaching serves the same dignity — prayer as personal communion with the Father, not a formula for extracting favors or a performance for crowds. There is a word here for communities that have carried more than their share of waiting: the twelve's devotion ran ahead of their understanding, loyalty first and comprehension slowly following, yet Jesus kept teaching them. And when the worst news arrives — John executed — the prepared company is ready to move, grief already gathered into resolve.
