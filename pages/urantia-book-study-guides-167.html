What happened during Jesus' visit to Philadelphia?

Paper 167 takes Jesus to Philadelphia, the Perean city where Abner's preaching has prepared a welcome. Over six hundred followers accompany him. A wealthy Pharisee who has accepted Abner's teachings hosts a Sabbath breakfast where a Jerusalem dignitary takes offense at losing the seat of honor, and a lawyer's pious remark draws from Jesus a searching parable. Jesus teaches in the synagogue — the first opened to him since the Sanhedrin closed them all — and on Sunday night a runner arrives from Bethany: Lazarus is very sick.

“No miracles had attended the recent preaching tour through the Decapolis, and, excepting the cleansing of the ten lepers, thus far there had been no miracles on this Perean mission. This was a period when the gospel was proclaimed with power, without miracles, and most of the time without the personal presence of Jesus or even of his apostles.”

What this paper teaches

The paper first explains the Perean pattern: as a rule only ten apostles traveled with Jesus, at least two remaining at Pella to instruct the multitude, while three to five hundred campers often followed him on the road. Philadelphia received him joyfully; James spoke in the synagogue Friday night, and David's messengers brought news of the kingdom's advance throughout Palestine and from Alexandria and Damascus. At the breakfast, about forty leading men and a few lawyers were present; only Jesus' enemies noted that he had not observed the ceremonial washing of hands. From Philadelphia the journey bends toward Judea: some fifty friends and enemies follow, and at Jericho his words about marriage and the blessedness of children draw scores of mothers with their children the next morning.

Why it matters

Honor is the quiet theme of this visit. A great man takes offense over a seat four places too far left, while the host's true guest pays the seating chart no mind. Jesus eats with Pharisees without flattering them, answers a lawyer's easy blessing with a parable his friendly host was compelled to take to heart, and lets children — not dignitaries — set the next morning's agenda at Jericho. For communities long ranked by tables they did not set, the lesson is plain: worth is not assigned by seating, and the kingdom keeps arriving through those the seating chart overlooks.
