Who were the shamans and medicine men?

As religious ritual grew more complex, this paper teaches, it came inevitably to be dominated by medicine men, shamans, and priests. Observances had progressed from placation, avoidance, and exorcism through coercion and conciliation to sacrifice, atonement, and redemption — and the spirit world was now regarded as unresponsive to ordinary mortals. Only the exceptional man or woman could catch the ear of the gods, and religion became secondhanded.

“THE evolution of religious observances progressed from placation, avoidance, exorcism, coercion, conciliation, and propitiation to sacrifice, atonement, and redemption. The technique of religious ritual passed from the forms of the primitive cult through fetishes to magic and miracles; and as ritual became more complex in response to man’s increasingly complex concept of the supermaterial realms, it was inevitably dominated by medicine men, shamans, and priests.”

“In the advancing concepts of primitive man the spirit world was eventually regarded as being unresponsive to the ordinary mortal. Only the exceptional among humans could catch the ear of the gods; only the extraordinary man or woman would be heard by the spirits. Religion thus enters upon a new phase, a stage wherein it gradually becomes secondhanded; always does a medicine man, a shaman, or a priest intervene between the religionist and the object of worship. And today most Urantia systems of organized religious belief are passing through this level of evolutionary development.”

What this paper teaches

The paper profiles the shaman as the ranking medicine man, ceremonial fetishman, and focus personality of evolutionary religion — in many groups outranking the war chief, sometimes functioning as priest and even priest-king, the office often hereditary. It describes spirit conjuring as a precise, complicated procedure, religion among ancient men as largely a technique for disease prevention in a life that was entirely prophylactic, and ritual whose essence was the perfection of its performance. It is frank that striking mental or physical abnormality — epilepsy, trance, fits — often qualified a person for the office.

Why it matters

Between the beginning of religion in fear and its consummation in the Father's all-powerful love, this paper says, stand the long ages of intermediaries. For communities whose access to God was policed — whose preaching was licensed, whose gatherings were surveilled — the warning and the hope are both here. The warning: no intermediary owns the ear of God. The hope: the ages of the shaman are a stage, not the destination; every believer's direct access to divine love is where the story is heading, and no gatekeeper can finally stand in that road.
