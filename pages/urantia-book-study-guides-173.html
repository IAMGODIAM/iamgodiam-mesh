Why did Jesus drive the money-changers from the temple?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 173 — Monday in Jerusalem · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

On Monday morning Jesus led the silent apostles down from Bethany to the temple, having told them to do no public teaching during Passover week. There he found the courts given over to a huge commercial traffic — money-changers, merchants, and cattle sellers — while the Sanhedrin met amid the noise. The leaders, overawed by Sunday's entry, did not dare arrest him. Instead they challenged his authority, and he answered with questions of his own and with parables that the crowd heard gladly.

“EARLY on this Monday morning, by prearrangement, Jesus and the apostles assembled at the home of Simon in Bethany, and after a brief conference they set out for Jerusalem. The twelve were strangely silent as they journeyed on toward the temple; they had not recovered from the experience of the preceding day. They were expectant, fearful, and profoundly affected by a certain feeling of detachment growing out of the Master’s sudden change of tactics, coupled with his instruction that they were to engage in no public teaching throughout this Passover week.”

“These temple money-changers not only conducted a regular banking business for profit in the exchange of more than twenty sorts of money which the visiting pilgrims would periodically bring to Jerusalem, but they also engaged in all other kinds of transactions pertaining to the banking business. Both the temple treasury and the temple rulers profited tremendously from these commercial activities. It was not uncommon for the temple treasury to hold upwards of ten million dollars while the common people languished in poverty and continued to pay these unjust levies.”

What this paper teaches

The paper follows the day from dread to confrontation. On the walk down Mount Olivet every apostle but Judas wondered what the Master would do; Judas alone wondered what he himself should do. Jesus began teaching about nine o'clock, then confronted the profiteering that had colonized the worship: the money-changers ran a full banking business, and the temple treasury and rulers profited tremendously from it. When the Pharisees demanded his authority and questioned him about John's mission, Jesus answered with the story of the two sons, and later, to the assembled crowd, the parable of the wedding feast.

Why it matters

The temple in this paper is a bank wearing sacred clothes: upwards of ten million dollars in the treasury while the common people languished in poverty and kept paying unjust levies. Jesus was not alone in resenting it — the people, especially pilgrims from foreign provinces, hated the profiteering too. That is the paper's charge to any age: when the house of God becomes a revenue machine, faith itself is taxed, and the poor pay the bill. Jesus' protest sides with the worshipers against the institution that was supposed to serve them.
