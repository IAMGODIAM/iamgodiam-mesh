What did Jesus say in his last discourse in the temple?

Shortly after two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, Jesus entered the temple with eleven apostles, Joseph of Arimathea, the thirty Greeks, and other disciples, and delivered his last public address. The paper says plainly what it was: his final appeal to the Jewish people and his last indictment of the enemies determined to destroy him. The court was quiet; the money-changers, driven out the day before, did not dare return. No one questioned him. He spoke, and then the public ministry was over.

“SHORTLY after two o’clock on this Tuesday afternoon, Jesus, accompanied by eleven apostles, Joseph of Arimathea, the thirty Greeks, and certain other disciples, arrived at the temple and began the delivery of his last address in the courts of the sacred edifice. This discourse was intended to be his last appeal to the Jewish people and the final indictment of his vehement enemies and would-be destroyers—the scribes, Pharisees, Sadducees, and the chief rulers of Israel. Throughout the forenoon the various groups had had an opportunity to question Jesus; this afternoon no one asked him a question.”

What this paper teaches

The discourse itself is a farewell with two edges. Jesus recounts his years of proclaiming the Father's love — the healings, the teaching, even the resurrection of the dead — and testifies that he and his apostles did their utmost to live in peace with their brethren and conform to the reasonable requirements of the law. There can be no peace, he says, between light and darkness, truth and error. Yet he also charges the rulers with a generational pattern: prophets sent and killed, from John's death at Herod's hands to the destruction now being prepared for the Son of Man. Afterward the Sanhedrin met in fateful session that evening.

Why it matters

Two things keep this indictment from becoming hatred of a people. First, the door stays open to the very end: even now, Jesus says, it is not too late for the leaders to turn and accept mercy. Second, the paper itself insists that the rulers' rejection “does not in any manner affect the status of any individual” — guilt belongs to choices, not to bloodlines or nations. For any community that has been collectively blamed, that sentence matters: heaven judges persons by their own turning, and no people is written off as a whole.
