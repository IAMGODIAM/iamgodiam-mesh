How did civilization begin among early mankind?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 68 — The Dawn of Civilization · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Civilization, Paper 68 teaches, is not inherited in the blood — it is a racial acquirement that every generation must be taught anew. The paper narrates the long forward struggle of the human species from a status little better than animal existence toward real, though imperfect, civilization: how primitive desires produced the first society, how ghost fear held it together, how the Dalamatia teachers nurtured mankind in group activities for three hundred thousand years, and how the customs of yesterday became the conventions of today.

“Civilization is a racial acquirement; it is not biologically inherent; hence must all children be reared in an environment of culture, while each succeeding generation of youth must receive anew its education. The superior qualities of civilization—scientific, philosophic, and religious—are not transmitted from one generation to another by direct inheritance. These cultural achievements are preserved only by the enlightened conservation of social inheritance.”

“Association early became the price of survival. The lone man was helpless unless he bore a tribal mark which testified that he belonged to a group which would certainly avenge any assault made upon him. Even in the days of Cain it was fatal to go abroad alone without some mark of group association. What this paper teaches

The paper is honest about human nature: primitive man was not naturally overflowing with brotherly feeling, and early races learned by sad experience that “in union there is strength.” Association became the price of survival — the lone man was helpless without a tribal mark testifying that his group would avenge him — and civilization became mankind’s insurance against violent death. It traces society’s foundations to land and nature: land is the stage of society and men are the actors, and man, a creature of the soil, cannot finally escape the land however earnestly he tries.

Why it matters

Hold onto this paper’s central claim: the superior qualities of civilization — scientific, philosophic, religious — are preserved only by the enlightened conservation of social inheritance. Nothing worth keeping passes itself down. A people whose schools, stories, and institutions were deliberately broken hears in this a commission, not a lament: rebuild the inheritance on purpose, teach each generation anew, and guard the teachers. The paper also names the cost honestly — civilization’s premiums are paid in submission to society’s demands — so the building must be done by the community’s own hands, on its own terms.
