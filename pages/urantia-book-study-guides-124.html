What was Jesus like as a schoolboy in Nazareth?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 124 — The Later Childhood of Jesus · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 124 covers Jesus' later childhood in Nazareth. He might have had better schooling in Alexandria, but Nazareth trained him by contact with all classes of people from every part of the civilized world and gave him a balanced view of Eastern and Western thought. He remained a favored pupil with one week of liberty each month, divided his time between trips with his father, his uncle's farm, and fishing out of Magdala — and at school dared to defend his drawing against the charge of idolatry.

“The most serious trouble as yet to come up at school occurred in late winter when Jesus dared to challenge the chazan regarding the teaching that all images, pictures, and drawings were idolatrous in nature. Jesus delighted in drawing landscapes as well as in modeling a great variety of objects in potter’s clay. Everything of that sort was strictly forbidden by Jewish law, but up to this time he had managed to disarm his parents’ objection to such an extent that they had permitted him to continue in these activities.”

What this paper teaches

The paper follows the boy year by year. It notes the minor ailments of childhood he shared with his brothers and baby sister. It tells of a July Sabbath stroll through the countryside with his father, when Jesus first expressed feelings showing his growing self-consciousness. It follows his trips to neighboring cities, sojourns on his uncle's farm south of Nazareth, and fishing excursions from Magdala. It records the school trouble over his landscapes and potter's-clay modeling, his sister Martha's birth, and Joseph building him a small workbench where he became expert in making yokes. It closes with his graduation from the synagogue schools, qualified to go up to Jerusalem.

Why it matters

The boy Jesus made art in a world that called it forbidden — landscapes and clay figures against strict religious law — and would not quietly surrender the gift. Yet the same household that worried over his drawing also built him a workbench and put tools of his own in his hands. For young people whose creativity is policed by rule or respectability, this paper dignifies both the artist's hand and the craftsman's bench. Formation happens where a child is free to question, to make things, and to grow — and wise parents learn to make room for the gift.
