How long did the age of mammals last on Urantia?

The mammalian era covers a little less than fifty million years, from the first appearance of the placental mammals to the end of the ice age. Paper 61 walks through this long Cenozoic story stage by stage: lands rising and seas retreating, the placental mammals suddenly appearing and spreading, the climate slowly cooling, the northern continents lifting to great heights, and finally the ice itself — the glacial age in which the great event of the era occurred, the evolution of primitive man.

“During this Cenozoic age the world’s landscape presented an attractive appearance—rolling hills, broad valleys, wide rivers, and great forests. Twice during this sector of time the Panama isthmus went up and down; three times the Bering Strait land bridge did the same. The animal types were both many and varied. The trees swarmed with birds, and the whole world was an animal paradise, notwithstanding the incessant struggle of the evolving animal species for supremacy.”

“Early in this period and in North America the placental type of mammals suddenly appeared, and they constituted the most important evolutionary development up to this time. Previous orders of nonplacental mammals had existed, but this new type sprang directly and suddenly from the pre-existent reptilian ancestor whose descendants had persisted on down through the times of dinosaur decline. The father of the placental mammals was a small, highly active, carnivorous, springing type of dinosaur.”

What this paper teaches

The paper divides the era into successive periods. It opens fifty million years ago, when the land areas stood generally above water and the placental mammals appeared suddenly from a small, active, carnivorous reptilian ancestor. Later periods trace the rapid evolution of placental forms, land elevation and sea segregation slowly cooling the world’s weather, and the preglacial uplift of North America, Europe, and Asia. The final sections follow the glacial period itself, when the action of the ice overshadowed every other phenomenon in the northern latitudes — and primitive man evolved.

Why it matters

This paper gives human origins a deep and honest timescale. Our line did not appear by shortcut; it emerged at the end of fifty million years of slow preparation — rising land, cooling climate, creatures learning to survive. That long view is itself a dignity teaching: the peoples of this world descend from ancestors who endured an ice age and kept going. No group alive today is closer to the beginning than another; all of us are the latest children of the same immense patience, and survival itself is our shared inheritance.
