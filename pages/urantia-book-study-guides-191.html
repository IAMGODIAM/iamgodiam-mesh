How did Jesus appear to the apostles and other leaders after the resurrection?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 191 — Appearances to the Apostles and Other Leaders · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 191 follows the risen Jesus as he reaches frightened people in different places: Simon Peter in a garden, the ten apostles behind barred doors, Thomas after a lonely week, Abner and Lazarus at Philadelphia, and Rodan with other believers at Alexandria. The paper begins with fear, grief, doubt, and argument on Resurrection Sunday, then shows recognition returning as the Master appears again and again to followers who felt scattered, endangered, and unsure.

“RESURRECTION Sunday was a terrible day in the lives of the apostles; ten of them spent the larger part of the day in the upper chamber behind barred doors. They might have fled from Jerusalem, but they were afraid of being arrested by the agents of the Sanhedrin if they were found abroad. Thomas was brooding over his troubles alone at Bethpage. He would have fared better had he remained with his fellow apostles, and he would have aided them to direct their discussions along more helpful lines.”

What this paper teaches

The opening section lingers in the upper chamber, where ten apostles hide from the Sanhedrin’s agents while John keeps insisting that Jesus had promised to rise again. Jesus then appears to Simon Peter in the garden of the Mark home, and later that evening to the assembled ten after Cleopas and Jacob have departed. Monday is spent with the morontia creatures present on the world. At Philadelphia the tenth manifestation reaches Abner, Lazarus, and about one hundred fifty others. Thomas, who has spent a lonesome week in the hills around Olivet, is met after his isolation, and on the road toward Galilee Jesus appears to Rodan and some eighty believers at Alexandria.

Why it matters

This paper honors the pace of people who cannot believe on command. The apostles are not scolded for barred doors; Jesus comes to Peter personally, to the group collectively, and to Thomas after his week alone, as if absence and doubt were also places where grace knows the address. For communities formed under threat, that matters: leadership here is not the loudest certainty but wounded people staying in the room, listening to one another, and letting hope re-enter without denying how terrible the day has been. Recognition becomes a shared discipline, not a private test.
