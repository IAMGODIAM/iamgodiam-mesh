What happened in the crisis at Capernaum?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 153 — The Crisis at Capernaum · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 153 records the turning point of Jesus' Galilean ministry. After the feeding of the five thousand and his refusal to be made king, he preaches an epoch-making sermon in the new Capernaum synagogue, reading from Deuteronomy and calling his hearers to choose whom they will serve. The sermon, and the hard questions that follow it, begin a crisis in the lives of the apostles that will last a whole year.

“ON FRIDAY evening, the day of their arrival at Bethsaida, and on Sabbath morning, the apostles noticed that Jesus was seriously occupied with some momentous problem; they were cognizant that the Master was giving unusual thought to some important matter. He ate no breakfast and but little at noontide. All of Sabbath morning and the evening before, the twelve and their associates were gathered together in small groups about the house, in the garden, and along the seashore. There was a tension of uncertainty and a suspense of apprehension resting upon all of them. Jesus had said little to them since they left Jerusalem.”

What this paper teaches

The paper shows the apostles waiting through a Sabbath of tension while Jesus, eating little, is occupied with a momentous problem. A distinguished congregation greets him at three o'clock in the new synagogue — Jairus having refused the Jerusalem leaders' pressure to bar him from speaking, answering that he would not violate his word. Jesus opens the sermon from the law in Deuteronomy, warns that many stand at the parting of the ways, and afterward answers many questions. In the after meeting a distraught youth is brought to him. The paper closes on the apostles' unmatched disappointment: again and again Jesus has dashed their fondest hopes, and now sorrow overtakes them as never before.

Why it matters

The crisis at Capernaum is what happens when a deliverer refuses the crown his followers want to force on him. Jesus will feed the hungry but will not be made king, and the same crowd that ate the loaves must now choose whom they will serve. Movements for freedom know this parting of the ways: the moment when the easy miracle is over and the hard teaching begins, when some who cheered turn back. This paper dignifies the choice itself. Faith that stays only for bread is exposed, and the disciples who remain do so because they have decided, not because they were carried by the multitude.
