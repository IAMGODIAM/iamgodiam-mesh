What do the world's religions teach about God?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 131 — The World's Religions · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 131 preserves a young man's homework: during the Alexandrian sojourn, Ganid spent his time and his father's money gathering what the world's religions teach about God and his relations with mortal man, using more than sixty learned translators. Jesus helped him make the selections. The paper's claim is striking — nearly all the monotheism in these teachings traces back, directly or indirectly, to the missionaries of Machiventa Melchizedek, who carried the doctrine of one God, the Most High, from Salem to the ends of the earth.

“God is supreme; he is the Most High of heaven and earth. God is the perfected circle of eternity, and he rules the universe of universes. He is the sole maker of the heavens and the earth. When he decrees a thing, that thing is. Our God is one God, and he is compassionate and merciful. Everything that is high, holy, true, and beautiful is like our God. The Most High is the light of heaven and earth; he is the God of the east, the west, the north, and the south.”

Those who love their fellows and who have pure hearts shall see God. God never forgets sincerity. He will guide the honest of heart into the truth, for God is truth.”

What this paper teaches

The paper walks through the compilation tradition by tradition. The Cynics, it says, best preserved the residual teachings of Melchizedek's disciples outside the Jewish religion. The Jewish selection came through the Kenites of Palestine, whose records the Jews preserved and modified. Ganid was shocked at Buddhism, which came so near to being a great and beautiful religion yet had no personal, universal God. Zoroaster had direct contact with descendants of the missionaries, and the one God became central in Persia. In India the Suduanists kept the teaching; a Far-Eastern religion and the religions of China carried it too. The paper ends with Ganid's own summary of belief, drawn from the whole collection.

Why it matters

For a Black-first study, this paper is a charter of religious dignity. It refuses the idea that truth about God belongs to one people: the same Most High is confessed by Cynics, Jews, Persians, Indians, and Chinese, because the teaching traveled to the ends of the earth. And the Cynic selection Ganid preserved states the social consequence plainly — the man who knows God looks upon all men as equal, as brethren. No tradition in this collection is treated as a mere error to be discarded; each is honored for the truth it kept. Faith that begins there cannot be used to rank peoples.
