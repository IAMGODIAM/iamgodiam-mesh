What happened at Caesarea-Philippi, and what did Peter confess?

Paper 157 brings Jesus and the twelve to Caesarea-Philippi, the capital of the Tetrarch Philip's domain, set in a charming valley between scenic hills beneath Mount Hermon. A planned meeting with his family at Capernaum has just been frustrated by Pharisees encamped on his mother's doorstep, and a temple-tax collector has raised the question of the temple tax with Peter. On the road, as they pause for lunch, Jesus suddenly confronts the twelve with the first question he ever addressed to them concerning himself: "Who do men say that I am?"

What this paper teaches

The paper first tells of the failed family appointment: Jude and Ruth repeatedly endeavored to elude the vigilance of the Pharisees and send word to Jesus, but in vain — again Jesus and his earth family failed to make contact. At Magadan Park more than one hundred believers gather before the company leaves early Tuesday morning for Caesarea-Philippi. There, under the mulberry trees, Jesus holds one of the most momentous sessions of his association with the chosen apostles. The new and vital feature of Peter's confession is the clear-cut recognition that Jesus is the Son of God, of unquestioned divinity. The paper ends with the apostles at the home of Celsus, waiting for messengers with funds, while Andrew holds a personal, searching conference with each of his brethren.

Why it matters

Before the confession comes the surveillance: a mother's house watched so closely that a son cannot come home. This paper holds that grief quietly beside its triumph — the family that cannot reach him, and the family of faith that finally names him. Peter's confession matters because it is recognition, not information; after long months of training, the apostles see who has been with them all along. And Jesus' charge, that they tell this to no man for the time being, keeps the truth from becoming a slogan before it can be lived. Some recognitions must be carried in silence until the carriers are ready.
