“THE ghost cult evolved as an offset to the hazards of bad luck; its primitive religious observances were the outgrowth of anxiety about bad luck and of the inordinate fear of the dead. None of these early religions had much to do with the recognition of Deity or with reverence for the superhuman; their rites were mostly negative, designed to avoid, expel, or coerce ghosts. The ghost cult was nothing more nor less than insurance against disaster; it had nothing to do with investment for higher and future returns.”

“In religion the negative program of ghost placation long preceded the positive program of spirit coercion and supplication. The first acts of human worship were phenomena of defense, not reverence. Modern man deems it wise to insure against fire; so the savage thought it the better part of wisdom to provide insurance against ghost bad luck. The effort to secure this protection constituted the techniques and rituals of the ghost cult.”

What this paper teaches

The paper describes a world organized around fear of the dead: death was dreaded because it liberated another ghost, and the ancients labored to send each ghost on its journey to deadland. Ghost placation long preceded spirit coercion and supplication; ancestor worship arose as the link between common ghosts and the higher spirits. Ghost fear is called the fountainhead of all world religion — spirits were imagined as having almost unlimited rights but no duties, while man had duties but no rights. Yet the cult persisted because it preserved moral sentiments and religious loyalties, and recognition of higher spirits gradually demanded higher spiritual methods.

Why it matters

The paper's sharpest insight is a justice claim: in the ghost world, power had rights without duties and people had duties without rights — exactly the arrangement of every slave society and every regime built on fear. Naming that pattern is the first step out of it. And there is tenderness here too: whole peoples paid heavy premiums of fear to protect their families from disaster. The ghost cult was a bitter school, but mankind's imagination cast off from its shores and never anchored again until it found a true God — fear was the tuition, not the destination.
