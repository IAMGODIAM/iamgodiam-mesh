Who was Rodan of Alexandria, and what did he teach?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 160 — Rodan of Alexandria · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 160 pauses the tour for a week of rest at Magadan — and gives the week to a philosopher. Rodan, a Greek from Alexandria who had recently become a disciple of Jesus through the teaching of one of Abner's associates, has come hoping the Master would talk over with him the harmonizing of his philosophy of life with Jesus' new religious teachings. The Master declines such a conference but receives him graciously, directing Nathaniel and Thomas to listen to all he has to say and tell him the gospel in return.

“Human life consists in three great drives—urges, desires, and lures. Strong character, commanding personality, is only acquired by converting the natural urge of life into the social art of living, by transforming present desires into those higher longings which are capable of lasting attainment, while the commonplace lure of existence must be transferred from one’s conventional and established ideas to the higher realms of unexplored ideas and undiscovered ideals.”

What this paper teaches

Early Monday morning Rodan begins a series of ten addresses to Nathaniel, Thomas, and a group of some two dozen believers who chanced to be at Magadan. He teaches that human life consists in three great drives — urges, desires, and lures — and that mortals may live together in just two ways: the material or animal way and the spiritual or human way. The effort toward maturity, he says, necessitates work, and work requires energy; while you have an eye single to the attainment of eternal realities, you must also make provision for the necessities of temporal living. In the final section Rodan takes up what he has been told: that the Master regards genuine human religion as the individual's experience with spiritual realities.

Why it matters

Rodan's confession is this paper's gift to every serious thinker who has stood where he stood. His philosophy gave him the urge to search for the realities of true attainment, the goal of maturity, he says, but the urge was impotent; the search lacked driving power; the quest suffered from the absence of certainty of direction. And these deficiencies, he testifies, have been abundantly supplied by the new gospel of Jesus, with its enhancement of insights, elevation of ideals, and settledness of goals. For people whose intellectual inheritance has been dismissed as worthless by every camp, this paper models another way: a philosopher is heard at length, honored in his search, and answered with the gospel in return.
