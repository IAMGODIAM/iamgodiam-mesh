How did the Melchizedek teachings spread through the Orient?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 94 — The Melchizedek Teachings in the Orient · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 94 follows the Salem missionaries as they carried Machiventa's gospel of one God and salvation through faith across the Eastern Hemisphere. Trained at centers from Salem, these teachers — recruited from many peoples and working largely through native converts — entered India, met the rising caste system, and watched their message mingle with Brahmanism. The paper then traces the teaching's later life in Buddhism, China, and Tibet, and reckons honestly with what was absorbed, altered, and lost along the way.

“The Salem missionaries preached the one God of Melchizedek, the Most High of heaven. This portrayal was not altogether disharmonious with the emerging concept of the Father-Brahma as the source of all gods, but the Salem doctrine was nonritualistic and hence ran directly counter to the dogmas, traditions, and teachings of the Brahman priesthood. Never would the Brahman priests accept the Salem teaching of salvation through faith, favor with God apart from ritualistic observances and sacrificial ceremonials.”

What this paper teaches

The paper opens with the missionary pattern itself: teachers trained at Salem centers, then pupils commissioned to teach their own peoples. In India it finds a cosmopolitan land under Aryan-Andite dominance, a Vedic cult consolidating under Brahman priest-teachers, and a polytheism already resynthesizing toward monotheism. Southward lay the Dravidian Deccan and an increasing caste system. The paper honors Brahmanism's highest phase as a noble reach of the mortal mind, then follows the centuries: Vedic ritual's return, Lao-tse and Confucius in China, Gautama's rise in India, the Refuge formula of Buddhism, and the strange blend preserved in Tibet.

Why it matters

The Salem pattern deserves attention: the missionaries did not rule the peoples they taught — they trained natives and commissioned them to teach their own. That is a picture of dignity in transmission, truth carried by a people's own sons and daughters. The paper is equally frank about the collision in India: a gospel of one God and one simple faith threatened a priesthood's ritual economy, and the priests refused it. Wherever access to God is fenced by caste or fee, this old story repeats itself — and so does the gospel that walks around the fence.
