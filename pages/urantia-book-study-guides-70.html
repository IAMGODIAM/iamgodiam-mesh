How did human government evolve?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 70 — The Evolution of Human Government · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Government, Paper 70 teaches, is not anyone’s invention — it is an unconscious development that evolved by trial and error because it had survival value. No sooner had man partially solved the problem of making a living than he faced the task of regulating human contacts: industry demanded law and order, and private property necessitated government. The paper follows that evolution from the horde, in which even children were common property, through the family, clan, and tribe, to the nation and the modern territorial state.

What this paper teaches

The paper’s judgments are blunt. War is the natural state and heritage of evolving man; peace is the social yardstick measuring civilization’s advancement — and in past ages a fierce war could institute social changes that would not have occurred naturally in ten thousand years. It traces the peace group from family to clan to tribe to nation, notes that effective state rule came only with a chief holding full executive authority, and records that social classes arise from the mental and physical inequality of human beings. On rights it is austere: nature confers no rights on man, only life and a world in which to live it, and natural justice is a man-made theory, not a reality. The great struggle throughout has concerned the concentration of power.

Why it matters

Read this paper as a mirror held up to every government a people has lived under. If peace is the yardstick of civilization, then a state that keeps its own citizens in a condition of war — by violence, by neglect, by design — measures itself, and the measurement is not flattering. And if nature confers no rights, then rights exist only where people build and defend them together; they are achievements of organized communities, not gifts expected from the powerful. That is why the concentration of power is named the great struggle: who holds it, and for whom, decides whether government serves life or preys on it.
