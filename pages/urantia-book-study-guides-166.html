What happened on Jesus' last tour of northern Perea?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 166 — Last Visit to Northern Perea · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 166 covers the tour of February 11 to 20, when Jesus and the twelve visited the cities and villages of northern Perea where Abner's associates and the women's corps were working. They found the messengers meeting with success — proof, Jesus kept pointing out, that the gospel can spread without miracles and wonders. At Ragaba a wealthy Pharisee named Nathaniel hosts a Sabbath breakfast; near Amathus they encounter ten lepers; at Gerasa a believing Pharisee asks how many will be saved.

“FROM February 11 to 20, Jesus and the twelve made a tour of all the cities and villages of northern Perea where the associates of Abner and the members of the women’s corps were working. They found these messengers of the gospel meeting with success, and Jesus repeatedly called the attention of his apostles to the fact that the gospel of the kingdom could spread without the accompaniment of miracles and wonders.”

What this paper teaches

The paper itself notes: the three months in Perea were carried on with little help from the twelve, and from this time the gospel reflected not so much Jesus' personality as his teachings — though his followers would later rebuild it around miraculous concepts and glorified memories. The Ragaba breakfast fills the first section: twenty Pharisees at Nathaniel's table, much whispering, lifted eyebrows, and sneering lips, until Jesus names what the invitation has become — an exhibition of ceremonial devotion to their own self-righteousness. Some who heard him believed and entered the kingdom; the larger number hardened and laid plans to trap his words. The tour continues past the ten lepers near Amathus and the exchange at Gerasa about the fewness of the saved, ending toward Philadelphia.

Why it matters

Twice this paper refuses the logic of display. The gospel spreads through villages without wonders, carried by messengers whose names history mostly did not keep; and at a rich man's table, Jesus exposes ceremonial devotion performed as self-righteousness — religion as a show for one's peers. Against both stands the quiet record: some at that very table believed and entered the kingdom. For people whose faithfulness goes unrecorded, northern Perea testifies that the kingdom needs no stage — and that even a staged breakfast can become a doorway for those who hear.
