What this paper teaches

The paper traces civilization from its cradle in southwestern Asia, where for some thirty-five thousand years after Adam the Nile valley, Arabia, Mesopotamia, and Turkestan formed its center, with climate as the decisive factor. It treats the tools of civilization as the foundation of cultural growth, and describes how climatic destruction of the Turkestan grasslands around 12,000 B.C. forced new industry and crude manufacturing. It follows the composite races across Eurasia, northern Africa, and the Pacific Islands at the dawn of history, and warns that biologic evolution and cultural civilization are not necessarily correlated — culture can decay even while organic evolution advances.

Why it matters

This paper refuses the lie that any people's rise is final or any people's fall is deserved. Civilization moved in waves and weakened as it spread; centers rose and crumbled while human worth stayed constant underneath. For communities told that history passed them by, the teaching cuts the other way: progress is an evolutionary inheritance belonging to the whole species, and it cannot be stopped — only delayed. Soil toil, once despised because slaves performed it, is named here the greatest of blessings; the labor of the lowly, not the glory of conquerors, is what actually carried civilization forward.
