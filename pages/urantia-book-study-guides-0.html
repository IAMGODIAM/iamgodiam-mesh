How does the Foreword ask us to read this book?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Foreword · Part I — The Central and Superuniverses · reviewed 2026-10-16

The Foreword is the book’s own instruction for reading it. It explains that mortals of Urantia are confused about the meanings of God, divinity, and deity, so the revealers begin by defining the word symbols the papers will use and outlining the meanings attached to Deity and universal reality. It is a definitive guide, not a finished statement by itself, and it asks to be read first — as map and dictionary — before the papers begin.

“IN THE MINDS of the mortals of Urantia—that being the name of your world—there exists great confusion respecting the meaning of such terms as God, divinity, and deity. Human beings are still more confused and uncertain about the relationships of the divine personalities designated by these numerous appellations. Because of this conceptual poverty associated with so much ideational confusion, I have been directed to formulate this introductory statement in explanation of the meanings which should be attached to certain word symbols as they may be hereinafter used in those papers which the Orvonton corps of truth revealers have been authorized to translate into the English language of Urantia.”

What this paper teaches

The introduction first admits the difficulty of enlarging concepts while restricted to a circumscribed language, allowing new terms only when English cannot carry the concept. It then places Urantia among many inhabited planets in the local universe of Nebadon, within the superuniverse of Orvonton, one of seven superuniverses circling the central universe of Havona and the Isle of Paradise. The definitions that follow move through Deity as divinely coordinated activity, God as the focus of mortal symbolizing, reality in seven phases and Absolutes, personality as deified reality, the sevenfold approach that compensates for creature limitation, the evolving Supreme and eventuating Ultimate, and the inevitable Paradise Trinity.

Why it matters

A book that begins by defining its terms is practicing a kind of respect. It does not use sacred vocabulary to confuse the reader or to keep power with interpreters; it slows down, names its limits, and offers a guide so ordinary mortals can follow. It also tells the people of one small world that their planet has a name, a place, and an address in a vast ordered creation. For readers whose language about God was handed down through coercion, that clarity is not cold — it is an invitation to think freely and worship honestly.
