How did Jesus begin his public work?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 141 — Beginning the Public Work · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 141 follows Jesus and the twelve as they leave Bethsaida in January of A.D. 27 and begin the public work in earnest. Rather than preaching openly, Jesus spends these weeks teaching crowds along the Jordan, tarrying near Pella and Amathus, and instructing his apostles in the new kingdom. Again and again he presses one idea on them: God is a Father, not a bookkeeper. The journey ends with a month near Bethany beyond Jordan before the climb to Jerusalem for the Passover.

“ON THE first day of the week, January 19, A.D. 27, Jesus and the twelve apostles made ready to depart from their headquarters in Bethsaida. The twelve knew nothing of their Master’s plans except that they were going up to Jerusalem to attend the Passover feast in April, and that it was the intention to journey by way of the Jordan valley. They did not get away from Zebedee’s house until near noon because the families of the apostles and others of the disciples had come to say good-bye and wish them well in the new work they were about to begin.”

What this paper teaches

The paper opens with the farewell at Zebedee's house and the departure south, a route chosen partly because Jesus' fame was spreading and Herod would soon take notice. Near Pella, where John had preached and baptized, crowds gathered from Galilee, Phoenicia, Syria, the Decapolis, Perea, and Judea, and the apostles made progress teaching John's disciples without detracting from John's preaching. At Amathus, over almost three weeks, Jesus instructed the apostles in the new concept of God, held an eventful conference on spiritual unity, and received Teherma, a Persian businessman from Damascus. Four weeks at Bethany beyond Jordan, with apostolic couples sent up to Jericho, close the paper before the ascent to Jerusalem.

Why it matters

This is a movement that begins quietly, among ordinary people saying good-bye at a fisherman's house. Its central insistence — God is a Father, not a great and supreme bookkeeper — refuses every religion of ledgers, debts, and graded worth; sonship is a relationship, not an account balance. The paper also models honor across lines: John's followers, who made up most of the early company, are received without erasing their teacher, and a Persian seeker is welcomed into the circle. A people building dignity from below can recognize the pattern: receive the predecessor's people with respect, welcome the stranger, and keep the teaching plain — God is a Father.
