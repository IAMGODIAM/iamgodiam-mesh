What was the Garden of Eden?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 73 — The Garden of Eden · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 73 explains how the Garden of Eden came to be. After the Caligastia downfall, Urantia's races fell into cultural decadence and spiritual poverty, though their physical development continued. Almost forty thousand years ago the Life Carriers judged that the races' biologic progress was nearing its apex, and with the Melchizedek receivers they petitioned the Most Highs of Edentia for biologic uplifters. Tabamantia inspected the planet and recommended Material Sons — and in a little less than one hundred years, Adam and Eve arrived.

“Tabamantia, sovereign supervisor of the series of decimal or experimental worlds, came to inspect the planet and, after his survey of racial progress, duly recommended that Urantia be granted Material Sons. In a little less than one hundred years from the time of this inspection, Adam and Eve, a Material Son and Daughter of the local system, arrived and began the difficult task of attempting to untangle the confused affairs of a planet retarded by rebellion and resting under the ban of spiritual isolation.”

What this paper teaches

The paper describes the Garden's long preparation. For almost one hundred years before Tabamantia's inspection, Van and his associates preached from their highland headquarters the advent of a promised Son of God, a racial uplifter. A committee spent almost three years choosing the location. The finished Garden was the abode of the Material Sons, marked by floral beauty and botanic grandeur, with the stone temple of the Universal Father at the center of the peninsula. There Van planted the tree of life, whose leaves were for 'the healing of the nations.' After Adam vacated it, the Garden passed to the Nodites, Cutites, and Suntites.

Why it matters

Notice who kept the Garden ready: Van and a faithful remnant preached and prepared for a century in a world written off as savage and stagnant, and their labor was not wasted. The Garden itself says something about heaven's priorities — the uplift of a whole people was housed in beauty, with a temple at the center and a tree whose leaves heal nations, not individuals only. For communities told their neighborhoods are beyond repair, Eden is the counter-witness: preparation is holy work, and the healing is meant for the nations — peoples, together.
