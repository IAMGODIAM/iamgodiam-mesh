What happened when Jesus went to the feast of dedication?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 164 — At the Feast of Dedication · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 164 recounts a secret journey: while the camp at Pella is being established, Jesus takes Nathaniel and Thomas up to Jerusalem for the feast of dedication. The two apostles protest the danger, but Jesus is determined to give the teachers in Israel one more opportunity to see the light before his hour comes. In the city he teaches believing leaders gathered at the home of Nicodemus, heals Josiah, a man blind from birth, and teaches in Solomon's Porch while the Sanhedrin debates his Sabbath-breaking.

“Jesus had attended the feast of tabernacles that he might proclaim the gospel to the pilgrims from all parts of the empire; he now went up to the feast of the dedication for just one purpose: to give the Sanhedrin and the Jewish leaders another chance to see the light. The principal event of these few days in Jerusalem occurred on Friday night at the home of Nicodemus. Here were gathered together some twenty-five Jewish leaders who believed Jesus’ teaching. Among this group were fourteen men who were then, or had recently been, members of the Sanhedrin. This meeting was attended by Eber, Matadormus, and Joseph of Arimathea.”

What this paper teaches

The paper opens with the apostles' failed remonstrance and Jesus' single reply, then moves to the Friday night meeting at Nicodemus' home: some twenty-five Jewish leaders who believed his teaching, fourteen of them present or recent members of the Sanhedrin, along with Eber, Matadormus, and Joseph of Arimathea — all amazed at the breadth of the Master's remarks. It then examines the teaching Nathaniel's question exposes: the rabbis held that blindness from birth was caused by sin — a father's, a mother's, or even the child's own before birth — and many in those regions tolerated the theory of reincarnation. The healing of Josiah raises such discussion that the Sanhedrin convenes on the Sabbath to deal with it.

Why it matters

At the center of this paper stands a cruel doctrine and its quiet undoing. The teachers of the day explained a man's blindness as punishment — his parents' sin, his mother's indulgence, even his own sin before birth. Such teaching always lands hardest on the afflicted, adding shame to suffering and blame to disability. Jesus answers not with a theory but with a healing, and the council's response is to prosecute the day on which it happened. The paper also shows who else was listening: respected leaders, even members of the Sanhedrin, gathered in Nicodemus' home because the light drew them too.
