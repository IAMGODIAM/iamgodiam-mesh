What this paper teaches

The paper begins with the carriers: Salem missionaries active across Europe, Cynic preachers of faith and trust in God still working in Roman Europe in the first century, and Jewish mercenary soldiers who spread the doctrine through the West's military struggles. Among the Greeks, an Aryan invasion's anthropomorphic gods nearly erased the Salem influence, and Zeus headed a pantheon of happy-go-lucky divinities the intelligent Greeks never took very seriously. The paper then turns to Latin religion's political character, the Greco-Roman turn to spectacular mystery cults — Phrygian, Egyptian, and finally Mithras — and the late arrival of personal religion as an independent institution.

Why it matters

Two carriers in this paper deserve a second look: wandering Cynic preachers who kept faith and trust in God alive without temples, and ordinary soldiers whose movements carried a doctrine farther than any pulpit. Truth, in this telling, travels with the unimportant. The paper's Greek lesson cuts the other way: a people can inherit a near-monotheism and trade it for gods made in their own image, gods they neither love nor fear. The warning is plain — when a community's God becomes merely its own reflection, worship has already been lost, however fine the temples look.
