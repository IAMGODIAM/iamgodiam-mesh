Why did Jesus visit Jerusalem quietly between the tours?

Paper 147 covers the weeks between the first and second Galilean tours: a return to Capernaum, two quiet weeks at Bethsaida, and a restrained Passover visit to Jerusalem. Jesus does no public preaching in the city — only private teaching and personal work — while crowds seeking healing, comfort, and salvation throng Bethany and Gethsemane. The paper also records the healing of the centurion Mangus's servant, the first bloodless Passover shared by all twelve, and the working cooperation between Jesus' apostles and John's.

During this period Jesus, accompanied by James and John Zebedee, made two secret trips to Tiberias, where they met with the believers and instructed them in the gospel of the kingdom.”

What this paper teaches

During two weeks at Bethsaida the apostles taught by the seaside while Jesus spent much time alone in the hills, twice slipping away with James and John to instruct believers at Tiberias. Before the journey south, Mangus, a Roman centurion, sought help for his servant, and Jesus marveled at the gentile's faith — not found so great in Israel. At Jerusalem the thronging crowds drove the party to tents at Gethsemane, and the apostles did no public preaching, only private teaching. The paper notes the first bloodless Passover eaten by Jesus and all twelve, while John's apostles kept the feast separately with Abner; the two groups remained cooperative and on the best of terms. A feast at Simon the Pharisee's home and the return by Jericho close the interlude.

Why it matters

A Roman officer's faith is praised above Israel's — the paper keeps breaking the boundary between insider and outsider, honoring trust wherever it appears. The Passover is reimagined bloodless, a table of fellowship rather than slaughter, and two companies that could have become rivals choose cooperation without demanding uniformity. There is a pattern here for movements that survive: share the work, refuse the feud, and let the table get wider. Even the crowds are met where they are — healing for bodies, comfort for minds, salvation for souls — with private teaching instead of spectacle.
