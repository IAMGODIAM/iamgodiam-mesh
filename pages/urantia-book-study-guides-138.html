How did Jesus train the kingdom's messengers?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 138 — Training the Kingdom's Messengers · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 138 follows the training of the first messengers. After preaching the sermon on “The Kingdom,” Jesus called the six apostles together and disclosed his plans for visiting the cities around and about the Sea of Galilee. The six were sent out to labor two weeks at a time, returning to Zebedee's home for conference, while Jesus visited family at Nazareth. Their first tour taught them the great value of direct, personal contact — and that religion is purely and wholly a matter of personal experience.

“This first missionary tour of the six was eminently successful. They all discovered the great value of direct and personal contact with men. They returned to Jesus more fully realizing that, after all, religion is purely and wholly a matter of personal experience. They began to sense how hungry were the common people to hear words of religious comfort and spiritual good cheer. When they assembled about Jesus, they all wanted to talk at once, but Andrew assumed charge, and as he called upon them one by one, they made their formal reports to the Master and presented their nominations for the six new apostles.”

What this paper teaches

The training was practical and costly. It began with a wound: James and Jude were hurt at not being called, and Jesus' refusal to place close relatives in the corps opened a gulf with his family that lasted through his ministry. The six toured, reported, and voted on nominations for six more — Matthew the customs collector, the Alpheus twins, Thomas, and Judas Iscariot among them. Then came a week of intense training, a planned five months of quiet personal work, common funds divided with provision for dependent families, and the grueling alternation of fishing and ministry by which the twelve were tested.

Why it matters

Notice how the kingdom's messengers were chosen: the first six nominated, the whole company voted, and Jesus gave every candidate the call in person. Leadership here is discerned in community, not seized or inherited — indeed, inheritance is exactly what Jesus refused when he passed over his own brothers. Notice also the economics: a common purse, divided into equal portions, with care for dependent families provided before the work began. A movement that trains its people slowly, chooses them openly, and feeds their families first has already preached its best sermon before a word was spoken.
