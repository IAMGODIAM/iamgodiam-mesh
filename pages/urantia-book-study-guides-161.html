What did Rodan debate with Nathaniel and Thomas about God?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 161 — Further Discussions with Rodan · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 161 continues the discussions with Rodan of Alexandria. The one point of disagreement was the personality of God: Rodan accepted everything presented about God's attributes, but argued that the Father in heaven cannot be a person as man conceives personality. Jesus refused to referee the argument, telling Thomas it matters little what idea of the Father one holds if one is spiritually acquainted with his infinite and eternal nature. Nathaniel finally reasoned Rodan into believing in the Father's personality.

“There was one matter on which Rodan and the two apostles did not see alike, and that was the personality of God. Rodan readily accepted all that was presented to him regarding the attributes of God, but he contended that the Father in heaven is not, cannot be, a person as man conceives personality. While the apostles found themselves in difficulty trying to prove that God is a person, Rodan found it still more difficult to prove he is not a person.”

What this paper teaches

The paper opens at Magadan on Sunday, September 25, A.D. 29, where Jesus surprises all by announcing the journey to Jerusalem for the feast of tabernacles; the evangelists are sent to visit the believers in Galilee and the women's corps back to Bethsaida. It takes up the debate on God's personality: Thomas argues that God communicates with man, Rodan answers that God does not reveal himself personally, and Nathaniel appeals to experience — which Rodan allows proves God's reality, though not yet his personality. Nathaniel's reasoning begins from the Trinity: the Father enjoys equality of communication with the Eternal Son and the Infinite Spirit. The paper turns to the divine nature of Jesus, and closes by tracing how consciousness of divinity grew in his mind up to his baptism.

Why it matters

This paper honors the honest questioner. Rodan is a Greek, an outsider to the apostles' faith, yet his argument is heard in full, answered with reason and lived experience rather than authority, and he is won — not shamed — into a larger faith. Jesus himself refuses to settle the matter by decree: what counts is not the concept a person can defend but spiritual acquaintance with the Father's nature. For communities whose beliefs were long policed by others, that is a liberating word: God is known by acquaintance, not by passing someone else's test of words.
