How did early religion evolve?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 86 — Early Evolution of Religion · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Early religion did not require revelation, this paper teaches. It grew from the primitive worship urge through the normal functioning of the human mind under the sixth and seventh mind-adjutants. Chance — so-called luck — gave it roots: for food hunters living on the ragged edge of existence, good luck and bad luck were constant powers, and anxiety and death slowly built the concepts of ghosts, spirits, and the soul.

“THE evolution of religion from the preceding and primitive worship urge is not dependent on revelation. The normal functioning of the human mind under the directive influence of the sixth and seventh mind-adjutants of universal spirit bestowal is wholly sufficient to insure such development.”

“Aside from the natural worship urge, early evolutionary religion had its roots of origin in the human experiences of chance—so-called luck, commonplace happenings. Primitive man was a food hunter. The results of hunting must ever vary, and this gives certain origin to those experiences which man interprets as good luck and bad luck. Mischance was a great factor in the lives of men and women who lived constantly on the ragged edge of a precarious and harassed existence.”

What this paper teaches

The paper traces religion's early steps: roots in chance and commonplace happenings, where a hunter's varying fortune taught the dread of bad luck; anxiety as the natural state of the savage mind; death as the supreme shock, the most perplexing combination of chance and mystery. From ghost dreams it derives the concept of a supermaterial phase of personality, and it follows the nonmaterial part of man through its names — ghost, spirit, shade, phantom, specter, and latterly soul. Man inherited a natural environment, acquired a social environment, and imagined a ghost environment, paying fear, superstition, and priest gifts as insurance premiums against ill luck.

Why it matters

Read with sympathy, this paper explains why religion took root so deeply among oppressed people: when life is governed by forces you cannot control — the master's whim, the market, the patrol — chance feels like a power and prayer feels like insurance. The paper does not sneer at that inheritance; it shows the dread of bad luck once paralyzed work and joy, and it honors the long climb from fear toward faith. Knowing religion began in anxiety frees the believer to grow past anxiety into trust. The ancestors who paid those premiums were buying safety for their children with the only currency they had.
