Who were the twelve apostles?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 139 — The Twelve Apostles · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 139 is a group portrait — one section for each of the twelve apostles. It opens with a paradox: although Jesus repeatedly dashed his apostles' hopes and tore to shreds their every ambition for personal exaltation, only one deserted him. The paper treats them neither as ignorant rustics nor as stained-glass saints: all but the Alpheus twins were graduates of the synagogue schools, laymen learned in the scriptures, and Jesus learned from them about human nature even as they learned the kingdom from him.

“The apostles learned from Jesus about the kingdom of heaven, and Jesus learned much from them about the kingdom of men, human nature as it lives on Urantia and on the other evolutionary worlds of time and space. These twelve men represented many different types of human temperament, and they had not been made alike by schooling. Many of these Galilean fishermen carried heavy strains of gentile blood as a result of the forcible conversion of the gentile population of Galilee one hundred years previously.”

“Do not make the mistake of regarding the apostles as being altogether ignorant and unlearned. All of them, except the Alpheus twins, were graduates of the synagogue schools, having been thoroughly trained in the Hebrew scriptures and in much of the current knowledge of that day. Seven were graduates of the Capernaum synagogue schools, and there were no better Jewish schools in all Galilee.”

What this paper teaches

The sections move man by man. Andrew of Capernaum, the oldest, chairs the corps. Simon Peter joined at thirty; James Zebedee, also thirty, was nicknamed a son of thunder; John, at twenty-four, was the youngest of the twelve. Philip, the fifth chosen, brought Nathaniel, the sixth and last chosen by the Master himself. Matthew was chosen by Andrew; Thomas by Philip. The Alpheus twins, James and Judas, fishermen near Kheresa, came ninth and tenth; Simon Zelotes eleventh; and Judas Iscariot, the twelfth, was chosen by Nathaniel. Again and again the chain is personal: one man brings another.

Why it matters

This portrait is a rebuke to every cult of the singular leader. The kingdom's first company is a chain of introductions — Andrew brings, Philip brings, Nathaniel brings — ordinary workingmen, a tax collector, a zealot, twins who were simply available. They were not the unlearned rabble of later condescension; the paper corrects that record directly. Nor were they chosen for polish: Jesus kept men whose ambitions he continually disappointed, and only one finally broke. For communities building leadership from the grassroots, the pattern holds: dignity is assumed, people are known one by one, and faithfulness outlasts ambition.
