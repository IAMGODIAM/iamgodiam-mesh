What does the revelation say about wealth built on oppression?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · The Justice Reader · reviewed 2026-09-19

It is unsparing: no one who knows God can engage in the oppressions of wealth; riches derived from the sweat of oppressed labor are “a moral curse and a spiritual stigma”; such wealth should be restored to those robbed or their descendants; and no enduring civilization can be built on defrauding the laborer of his hire.

““No mortal who knows God and seeks to do the divine will can stoop to engage in the oppressions of wealth. Riches are a moral curse and a spiritual stigma when they are derived from the sweat of oppressed mortal man. All such wealth should be restored to those who have thus been robbed or to their children and their children’s children. An enduring civilization cannot be built upon the practice of defrauding the laborer of his hire.”

Abba Talk corpus · 132:5.18 · read the passage

Restoration, named plainly

Read the sentence slowly: “All such wealth should be restored to those who have thus been robbed or to their children and their children’s children.” That is restitution across generations — what the modern world calls reparations — stated as a spiritual requirement, not a policy proposal. The text does not hedge it with feasibility studies. It treats the return of stolen wealth as a condition of moral sanity: while the theft stands unrepaired, the civilization built on it cannot endure.

The laborer and the hire

The closing line reaches back to an ancient standard — the laborer defrauded of his hire — and makes it the load-bearing test of civilization itself. Apply it as a measuring stick: wage theft, prison labor, sharecropping’s long shadow, the racial wealth gap compounded over centuries. The passage does not ask whether these arrangements are legal or customary. It asks whether they are enduring — and answers that nothing built on defrauded labor can be. What cannot endure will, in time, fall. The only question is what we build in its place.
