What did Jesus teach about citizenship on his last free day?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 178 — Last Day at the Camp · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Thursday was, the paper says, Jesus' last free day on earth as a divine Son incarnate in the flesh, and he spent it teaching. In a secluded spot above the Gethsemane camp he gave his farewell address to the combined company of apostles and chosen disciples, Jews and gentiles — all twelve present save Judas. For almost two hours he answered their questions about how the kingdom of heaven relates to the kingdoms of this world, and how sons of God should live as citizens of earthly governments.

“As mortal and material men, you are indeed citizens of the earthly kingdoms, and you should be good citizens, all the better for having become reborn spirit sons of the heavenly kingdom. As faith-enlightened and spirit-liberated sons of the kingdom of heaven, you face a double responsibility of duty to man and duty to God while you voluntarily assume a third and sacred obligation: service to the brotherhood of God-knowing believers.”

“You may not worship your temporal rulers, and you should not employ temporal power in the furtherance of the spiritual kingdom; but you should manifest the righteous ministry of loving service to believers and unbelievers alike. In the gospel of the kingdom there resides the mighty Spirit of Truth, and presently I will pour out this same spirit upon all flesh. The fruits of the spirit, your sincere and loving service, are the mighty social lever to uplift the races of darkness, and this Spirit of Truth will become your power-multiplying fulcrum.”

What this paper teaches

The address, as the paper restates it, lays down a double citizenship. Believers are citizens of earthly kingdoms and should be good ones — all the better for being reborn sons of the heavenly kingdom — facing a double responsibility of duty to man and duty to God, plus a third sacred obligation: service to the brotherhood of believers. They may not worship temporal rulers, nor use temporal power to advance the spiritual kingdom; instead they serve believers and unbelievers alike with loving ministry. The paper admits few hearers grasped even part of it; that afternoon Jesus led the twelve over Olivet toward Jerusalem.

Why it matters

This is a charter for people who must live under governments that do not love them. It refuses both idolatry and withdrawal: no worship of rulers, no seizure of state power for the kingdom — and no retreat from honest citizenship either. The lever of change named here is “sincere and loving service,” called a mighty social lever to uplift the races of darkness. And the paper dares a long promise: persistent preaching of this gospel will one day bring nations “a new and unbelievable liberation, intellectual freedom, and religious liberty.”
