What are the social problems of religion?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 99 — The Social Problems of Religion · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 99 argues that religion does its highest social work precisely when it is least entangled with society's secular institutions. For ages, religion's task was replacing evil with good inside the existing order — which meant it quietly tended to preserve that order. But mechanical inventions and spreading knowledge are now forcing continuous social reconstruction, and the paper asks what religion must become: not the architect of new social orders, but a forceful influence for moral stability and spiritual progression amid unending change.

“RELIGION achieves its highest social ministry when it has least connection with the secular institutions of society. In past ages, since social reforms were largely confined to the moral realms, religion did not have to adjust its attitude to extensive changes in economic and political systems. The chief problem of religion was the endeavor to replace evil with good within the existing social order of political and economic culture. Religion has thus indirectly tended to perpetuate the established order of society, to foster the maintenance of the existent type of civilization.”

“But religion should not be directly concerned either with the creation of new social orders or with the preservation of old ones. True religion does oppose violence as a technique of social evolution, but it does not oppose the intelligent efforts of society to adapt its usages and adjust its institutions to new economic conditions and cultural requirements.”

What this paper teaches

The paper is blunt about institutional religion: because it has become an organic part of the social and economic order it should transform, it cannot supply the inspiration and leadership the coming reconstruction requires. Early Christianity, by contrast, was entirely free of civil entanglements, social commitments, and economic alliances. The paper then describes genuine religion's social fruit — it renders the religionist socially fragrant and creates insights into human fellowship — and religion's social corollary: knowing God as Father entails knowing man as brother. It names sectarianism a disease of institutional religion and dogmatism an enslavement of the spiritual nature, while urging that religion never hinder the social coordination of human institutions.

Why it matters

Few papers speak more directly to a church-born liberation tradition. The warning lands first: a religion fused with the economic order will bless that order, even when the order is unjust — institutional comfort is the enemy of prophetic sight. Yet the paper does not counsel withdrawal. Knowing God as Father carries a social corollary, knowing man as brother, and that fellowship is meant to make believers “socially fragrant” inside the common life. The task is delicate and familiar: stay free enough from the powers to tell the truth, and close enough to the people to serve the reconstruction.
