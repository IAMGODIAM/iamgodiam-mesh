What was the world like when Jesus was born?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 121 — The Times of Michael's Bestowal · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 121 sets the stage for the life of Jesus. A secondary midwayer who once attended the Apostle Andrew tells the story, working from existing records under a commission of midwayers. Jesus did not arrive in an age of spiritual decadence: the world was stirring with revived spiritual thinking, the Mediterranean lands formed one unified empire of roads and trade, and the Jewish people stood at a strategic crossroads of that world, settled in their sense of origin, history, and destiny.

“When Jesus was born, the entire Mediterranean world was a unified empire. Good roads, for the first time in the world’s history, interconnected many major centers. The seas were cleared of pirates, and a great era of trade and travel was rapidly advancing. Europe did not again enjoy another such period of travel and trade until the nineteenth century after Christ.”

“The Jews were a part of the older Semitic race, which also included the Babylonians, the Phoenicians, and the more recent enemies of Rome, the Carthaginians. During the fore part of the first century after Christ, the Jews were the most influential group of the Semitic peoples, and they happened to occupy a peculiarly strategic geographic position in the world as it was at that time ruled and organized for trade.”

What this paper teaches

The paper surveys the whole setting before the narrative begins. It describes the social and economic condition of the Roman state and the widespread peace that made the bestowal propitious. It traces the Jewish people — their Semitic roots among Babylonians, Phoenicians, and Carthaginians, and their peculiarly strategic geographic position. It weighs the gentiles, whose nobler hearts held abundant soil of natural goodness, and surveys the religions of the Occident, where religion had chiefly been an affair of tribe or nation. It notes how Greek culture and philosophy had reshaped Jewish religious thought in Jerusalem, and closes by explaining the written records this narrative draws upon.

Why it matters

This paper quietly overturns the idea that heaven waits for comfortable times. Jesus was born under an empire where a small upper class was rich and the rank and file of humanity languished in squalor and poverty, with no prosperous middle class to speak of. The revelation treats a colonized people at a trade crossroads — not Rome's elite — as the chosen ground of God's arrival. For communities that still live under powers they did not choose, that is a word of dignity: a people's expectancy, history, and faith under pressure are precisely where heaven chooses to begin.
