As Jesus and his accusers started toward Herod, the Master sent John back to Bethany: you can do no more for me—bring my mother to see me before I die. John hastened to the house of Martha and Mary, where the family waited, and Mary set out at once for the city, her daughter Ruth and son Jude with her. By the time they reached Jerusalem, Jesus had already been turned over to the Roman soldiers who would crucify him, and Judas had thrown his thirty pieces of silver across the temple floor.

What this paper teaches

The paper interweaves several threads. John carries the Master's request to Bethany while messengers bring the family hourly reports of the trial. About half past eight, Pilate's hearing ended and Jesus passed into the custody of the soldiers appointed to crucify him. Judas, dismissed by the rulers with contempt, scattered the silver in the temple and went out alone to face the true nature of his sin. A detachment of temple guards hurried to Gethsemane to disperse or arrest the followers. And the paper reflects that Jesus, arrested, knew his earthly work was finished and was little concerned with the details of his trials.

Why it matters

Two figures carry this paper's weight. Mary walks toward the city to see her son before he dies—the family of the condemned refusing to let him face execution unwitnessed, as families still gather outside prisons and courtrooms. And Judas discovers that the men who purchased his betrayal will not share his guilt: “What is that to us? See you to that.” Systems that buy informers discard them without mercy. Against both stands Pilate washing his hands, the eternal gesture of officials who call their cowardice neutrality. This paper lets no one who touched the machinery claim clean hands.
