What is the kingdom of heaven, as Jesus taught it?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 170 — The Kingdom of Heaven · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 170 gathers Jesus' last sermon at Pella, preached Saturday afternoon, March 11 — a full and complete discussion of the kingdom of heaven meant to clear up his followers' confusion. The kingdom, he teaches, must begin with the truth of the fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of man; it calls for a righteousness acquired by faith, exceeding the slavish works of the scribes and Pharisees; and though Jesus never gave it a precise definition, it is no earthly kingdom and no temporal throne.

What this paper teaches

The paper first sorts the inherited concepts: throughout the Hebrew scriptures there was a dual concept of the kingdom, one side of it a future hope to be realized in fullness upon the Messiah's appearance — the kingdom concept John the Baptist taught. From the very first, Jesus and the apostles taught both of these concepts, while the Persian teachings added the picture of a divine kingdom established by the triumph of good over evil at the end of the world. Jesus then centers the kingdom in the dual truth of God's fatherhood and man's brotherhood and demands a faith-righteousness beyond slavish works; the paper closes by tracing later ideas that attached themselves to the concept and forecasting the kingdom's development as related to the later Christian church.

Why it matters

Every earthly kingdom sorts people into rulers and ruled, insiders and outsiders. The kingdom of this sermon begins somewhere else entirely: one Father, and therefore one brotherhood of man. Its entry is by faith, not by bloodline, nation, or the slavish performance of works — the very heritage Jesus took up had been confused into a hope for one people's triumph, and he refused to leave it there. For communities living under kingdoms not their own, the teaching plants a citizenship no throne can grant and no throne can revoke, and measures righteousness by faith-filled living rather than displayed obedience.
