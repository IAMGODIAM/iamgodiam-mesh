What happened while Jesus' body lay in the tomb?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 188 — The Time of the Tomb · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

The rulers of the Jews had planned to throw Jesus' body into the open burial pits of Gehenna, exposed to wild beasts, as was customary for the crucified. Instead, Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus claimed it. At about half past four the burial procession started from Golgotha—the body wrapped in a linen sheet, carried by Joseph, Nicodemus, John, and the Roman centurion, followed by the faithful women watchers from Galilee. In a chamber tomb about ten feet square they prepared the body hurriedly, for the Sabbath was drawing on.

“They carried the body into the tomb, a chamber about ten feet square, where they hurriedly prepared it for burial. The Jews did not really bury their dead; they actually embalmed them. Joseph and Nicodemus had brought with them large quantities of myrrh and aloes, and they now wrapped the body with bandages saturated with these solutions. When the embalming was completed, they tied a napkin about the face, wrapped the body in a linen sheet, and reverently placed it on a shelf in the tomb.”

What this paper teaches

The paper is honest about its limits: the thirty-six hours between death and resurrection are little known, and it narrates what can be told—the embalming with myrrh and aloes, the bandages, the napkin about the face, the linen sheet, and the great doorstone rolled before the entrance while the women lingered until dark. It notes that Jesus' enemies remembered his promise to rise on the third day even when most disciples did not, that the disciples spent the Sabbath in hiding while Jerusalem discussed the death, and it closes by interpreting the cross: not an atonement for racial guilt, but the supreme devotion of the true shepherd for his flock.

Why it matters

Everything in this paper turns on who claims the body. The state and the council planned an open pit and scavengers—the final erasure of a condemned man's dignity—and two council members themselves defected from the plan to give him an honorable tomb. Communities that have fought to recover and bury their executed dead will feel the weight of that act. The paper also refuses to let the cross be read as a Father demanding suffering: Jesus did not die to appease an offended God. The cross is devotion, the shepherd's love measured in full—not heaven's violence against its own son.
