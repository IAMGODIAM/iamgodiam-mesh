What are fetishes, charms, and magic?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 88 — Fetishes, Charms, and Magic · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Fetishism, this paper teaches, is the ancient doctrine that a spirit can enter an inanimate object, an animal, or a human being — and the savage, logically enough, worshiped not the object but the spirit resident in it. Chance gave origin to many fetishes: whatever was extraordinary — a marked pebble, a comet, a recovery from sickness — was likely to be charged with spirit power and treasured as a charm.

“THE concept of a spirit’s entering into an inanimate object, an animal, or a human being, is a very ancient and honorable belief, having prevailed since the beginning of the evolution of religion. This doctrine of spirit possession is nothing more nor less than fetishism. The savage does not necessarily worship the fetish; he very logically worships and reverences the spirit resident therein.”

“Primitive man always wanted to make anything extraordinary into a fetish; chance therefore gave origin to many. A man is sick, something happens, and he gets well. The same thing is true of the reputation of many medicines and the chance methods of treating disease. Objects connected with dreams were likely to be converted into fetishes. Volcanoes, but not mountains, became fetishes; comets, but not stars. Early man regarded shooting stars and meteors as indicating the arrival on earth of special visiting spirits.”

What this paper teaches

The paper follows fetishism through all the primitive cults, from sacred stones — a string of beads was once a battery of charms — through idolatry, cannibalism, nature worship, and totemism. It notes the ghosts' supposed preference for objects they had owned in the flesh, tree and plant fetishes that became food taboos, and animals made sacred, like the dog of the Parsees. Against the ghost environment, savage man attempted magic — wands, medicine ritual, and incantations — while civilized man attacks the problems of a real environment through science.

Why it matters

Before dismissing charms and sacred objects, this paper asks for logic and respect: our ancestors reasoned carefully from the world they knew, and their stones, beads, and taboos carried memory, identity, and protection for the community. Colonizers mocked these same objects as proof of inferiority while looting them into museums. The paper restores the honest frame — fetishism was an honorable early chapter of religion itself, found on every continent. A people's treasured objects are not evidence against their humanity; they are among its earliest signatures. To study them seriously is to recover a chapter of our own story that contempt tried to erase.
