What does religion do in human experience?

Paper 100 describes what living religion actually does to a person: the experience of dynamic religious living transforms the mediocre individual into a personality of idealistic power. Religion, it says, is not a technique for attaining a static, blissful peace of mind, but an impulse for organizing the soul for dynamic service. Growth is the paper's keynote — of meanings, values, and loyalty — fostered in each individual, augmented through all, and visible in the spiritual fruit a life bears for its fellows.

What this paper teaches

The paper makes growth its measure. Religion produces growth of meanings and enhancement of values, while evil results when personal evaluations are elevated to absolutes; maturity substitutes higher meanings for personal pleasure. Spiritual development requires both a living connection with true spiritual forces and the continuous bearing of spiritual fruit for one's fellows. Children, it insists, must be allowed to grow their own religious experience rather than receive a ready-made adult one. The paper also surveys the lost souls wandering among the isms and cults of a frustrated age, compares evolutionary and revelatory religion, and closes with the attainable perfection of character modeled by Jesus of Nazareth.

Why it matters

This paper dignifies ordinary growth. Its test of faith is not a product — not vocabulary, membership, or performance — but progress: enhanced ideals, deepened values, and loyalty that keeps enlarging. That measure honors the slow sanctification of people the world calls mediocre, and it carries a tender instruction for how a community raises its young: give every child room to grow a faith of his own instead of forcing a ready-made adult experience on him. A people that has survived by growing under pressure can claim this paper's promise — devoted living is creative living, and growth itself is the witness.
