How did Jesus enter Jerusalem for the last time?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 172 — Going into Jerusalem · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Jesus and the apostles reached Bethany on Friday afternoon, March 31, and lodged with Simon, the village's leading citizen, while crowds came daily to see Lazarus, who had been raised from the dead. That weekend the whole town feasted Jesus in open defiance of the Sanhedrin's death decree. On Sunday morning he gave the twelve their final instructions in Simon's garden, and that afternoon he rode into Jerusalem — not, as his Bethany friends understood, as the kind of king the crowds expected.

“JESUS and the apostles arrived at Bethany shortly after four o’clock on Friday afternoon, March 31, A.D. 30. Lazarus, his sisters, and their friends were expecting them; and since so many people came every day to talk with Lazarus about his resurrection, Jesus was informed that arrangements had been made for him to stay with a neighboring believer, one Simon, the leading citizen of the little village since the death of Lazarus’s father.”

What this paper teaches

The paper moves from welcome to procession. It records the question on every lip — “will Jesus come up to the feast?” — and the perplexity of the chief priests, glad to have him within reach yet disconcerted by his boldness. At Simon's banquet, tendered in defiance of the Sanhedrin, Martha served while Mary watched among the women. On Sunday Jesus talked with Simon about Joshua and the walls of Jericho, then made ready at half past one; the Alpheus twins afterward returned the donkey to its owner while Jesus and the ten strolled through the temple, viewing the Passover preparations.

Why it matters

Bethany's welcome is this paper's quiet defiance: a whole village feasting a man under a death decree, in the open, with the Sanhedrin's agents present but afraid to act among his friends. Against that stands Jesus' own definition of his entry — not walls of brick and stone, but the crumbling of “the walls of prejudice, self-righteousness, and hate” before the preaching of the Father's love for all men. The procession into the city is therefore not a bid for a throne. It is love, publicly practiced, walking straight at the walls that keep people apart.
