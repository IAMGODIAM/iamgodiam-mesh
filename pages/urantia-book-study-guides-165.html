How did the Perean mission begin?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 165 — The Perean Mission Begins · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 165 opens the Perean mission, the last ministry of the Master. On Tuesday, January 3, A.D. 30, Abner gives the seventy their final instructions and sends them to all the cities and villages of Perea, a tour of almost three months. Jesus divides his time between the camp at Pella, where the gathered multitude grows past twelve hundred, and trips with the twelve to assist the seventy. The women's corps, now sixty-two strong, takes over most of the ministration to the sick.

“Throughout this tour of Perea the women’s corps, now numbering sixty-two, took over most of the work of ministration to the sick. This was the final period of the development of the higher spiritual aspects of the gospel of the kingdom, and there was, accordingly, an absence of miracle working. No other part of Palestine was so thoroughly worked by the apostles and disciples of Jesus, and in no other region did the better classes of citizens so generally accept the Master’s teaching.”

What this paper teaches

The paper names the working field: town after town across Perea — Zaphon, Gadara, Gerasa, Philadelphia, Heshbon, and some fifty additional villages — in a province about equally gentile and Jewish, called by the Jews the land beyond the Jordan. Under Abner's instructions the seventy baptized all believers, although Jesus had not so charged them. At Pella a daily rhythm forms: Jesus teaches the multitude in the morning, Peter and the other apostles teach each afternoon, and the evenings are reserved for questions and answers with the twelve and advanced disciples, groups averaging about fifty. By mid-March, over four thousand are hearing the morning preaching.

Why it matters

Notice what carries this mission: not spectacle. The paper stresses an absence of miracle working — the kingdom advances by teaching, baptizing, and tending the sick, with a women's corps of sixty-two doing most of the ministration. The famous teacher is often absent from the field; the work belongs to Abner's seventy and the unnamed believers of fifty villages. For communities whose real power has always been organized care — visiting the sick, teaching the children, feeding the gathered crowd — Perea is a mirror: heaven counts that faithfulness as the mission itself, not as support for somebody else's.
