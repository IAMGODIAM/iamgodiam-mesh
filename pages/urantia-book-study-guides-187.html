What this paper teaches

The paper follows the execution in sequence: the procession by the direct road through the Damascus gate, the soldiers binding the Master's arms to the crossbeam and nailing his hands to the wood, and Jesus hung upon the cross at about half past nine. It records the exchange between the two brigands—one railing at Jesus, the other, who had often heard the Master teach, answering him. Shortly after noon the sky darkened with fine sand in the air, and in that darkness, about half past three, David Zebedee sent out the last messengers carrying the news of the Master's death.

Why it matters

Crucifixion was execution as public theater, staged on a road where the wealthy passed to their villas, and this paper does not let the reader look away from who watches, who protests the wording of a sign, and who simply does the nailing. For peoples whose dead have been displayed by mobs and states as warnings, the scene needs no translation. Its center is a free act: Jesus declares that no one takes his life—he lays it down, and will take it up again. Even here, the condemned man is not only a victim; he is a shepherd keeping authority over his own gift.
