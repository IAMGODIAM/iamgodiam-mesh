Where did sin, sacrifice, and atonement come from?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 89 — Sin, Sacrifice, and Atonement · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Primitive man regarded himself as in debt to the spirits and in need of redemption, this paper teaches — and from that felt debt grew the doctrines of sin and salvation. The soul was seen as coming into the world under forfeit, as original sin, and it had to be ransomed: a scapegoat must be provided, a substitute offered for one's own life. Taboo, renunciation, and sacrifice all developed as ways of settling that account.

“PRIMITIVE man regarded himself as being in debt to the spirits, as standing in need of redemption. As the savages looked at it, in justice the spirits might have visited much more bad luck upon them. As time passed, this concept developed into the doctrine of sin and salvation. The soul was looked upon as coming into the world under forfeit—original sin. The soul must be ransomed; a scapegoat must be provided. The head-hunter, in addition to practicing the cult of skull worship, was able to provide a substitute for his own life, a scapeman.”

What this paper teaches

The paper traces the long ladder of atonement: taboo observance as the earliest societal regulation and ancestor of self-control; renunciation and fasting; sacrifice in its many forms; and the dark chapters of cannibalism and human sacrifice, which it treats as part of early society's mores rather than mere horror. It credits Moses with attempting to end human sacrifice by inaugurating the ransom as substitute, describes temple prostitution and sacrificial redemption as modifications of human sacrifice, and shows the practice advancing toward higher and more symbolic levels — until ancient man could conceive of favor with God attained only through sacrifice.

Why it matters

This paper lets a community name its own history honestly: the scapegoat is not an abstraction for peoples who have been made to carry other men's sins. The long evolution it traces — from human victims to substitutes to symbolic offerings — is a record of humanity slowly refusing to buy peace with somebody else's body. The old commandments are praised here as emancipating because they replaced thousands of crushing taboos and promised something in return for obedience: law that liberates instead of only forbidding is itself a milestone on the road out of fear.
