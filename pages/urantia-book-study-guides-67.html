What was the planetary rebellion, and what did it cost Urantia?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 67 — The Planetary Rebellion · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

After three hundred thousand years in charge of Urantia, Caligastia betrayed his trust and joined the Lucifer rebellion. Paper 67 tells how it happened — Satan’s inspection visit, the proposed Declaration of Liberty, the Prince’s premeditated agreement to betray the planet — and what followed: the roll call of the staff, in which Van and his court remained loyal; almost fifty years of confusion around Dalamatia; and the loyalists’ withdrawal to the highlands west of India to plan the world’s rehabilitation.

“THE problems associated with human existence on Urantia are impossible of understanding without a knowledge of certain great epochs of the past, notably the occurrence and consequences of the planetary rebellion. Although this upheaval did not seriously interfere with the progress of organic evolution, it did markedly modify the course of social evolution and of spiritual development. The entire superphysical history of the planet was profoundly influenced by this devastating calamity.”

“In all the administrative work of a local universe no high trust is deemed more sacred than that reposed in a Planetary Prince who assumes responsibility for the welfare and guidance of the evolving mortals on a newly inhabited world. And of all forms of evil, none are more destructive of personality status than betrayal of trust and disloyalty to one’s confiding friends. In committing this deliberate sin, Caligastia so completely distorted his personality that his mind has never since been able fully to regain its equilibrium.”

What this paper teaches

The paper insists that Urantia’s problems cannot be understood without this epoch: the rebellion barely touched organic evolution but markedly modified social evolution and spiritual development. It portrays Satan as no caricature but a brilliant creature of light, and records the Creator Son’s contempt for a betrayal planned in advance. It defines its terms carefully — sin as purposeful resistance to divine reality, iniquity as open, persistent defiance bordering on cosmic insanity — and warns that repeated, habitual sin can harden into iniquity. It closes by honoring Amadon, portrayed on Salvington as the outstanding hero of the whole system for rejecting the rebellion’s slogans.

Why it matters

This is the paper for everyone who has asked why the world handed to them is broken. Its answer is not that the victims failed, but that a trusted ruler betrayed his trust at the top, and the confusion rolled downhill for millennia. Yet the roll call matters as much as the betrayal: when the staff was counted, Van’s whole court had remained loyal, and ordinary Amadon outshone every brilliant rebel. The teaching for a betrayed people is double — name the treachery honestly, and build with the loyal remnant that withdrew, regrouped, and planned the rehabilitation.
