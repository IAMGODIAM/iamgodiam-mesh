What this paper teaches

The paper is candid about marriage's unromantic beginnings: it was not founded on sex relations, which were incidental, nor on any biologic urge in man — food hunger first drew savage man to woman and the shelter of her children. It traces the blood-bond concept of human relationship, woman's compelled position through male strength and the mores, and proposes woman's status as a fair criterion of marriage's evolutionary progress in any age. It names the handicap of enforced maternity, the reproductive urge that brings couples together without insuring cooperation, and warns that the rising tide of self-gratification is the great threat against family life.

Why it matters

This paper measures a civilization by how it treats its women and its homes — a standard under which slaveholding and segregated societies condemn themselves. It says plainly that in self-perpetuation woman is man's equal, and that her disadvantages were imposed by force and custom, not by nature. For a people whose family life was systematically attacked, the teaching that the family is the channel of culture itself dignifies what grandmothers and aunties held together against every pressure: the home was never a private retreat from history; it was where history was preserved.
