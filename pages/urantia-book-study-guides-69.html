Where did human institutions come from?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 69 — Primitive Human Institutions · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Man, Paper 69 says, shows his superiority over his animal ancestors as a toolmaker, a communicator, and an institution builder — and emotionally in his ability to appreciate humor, art, and religion. The paper traces how institutions grew: primitive industry as insurance against famine, the division of labor first by natural and then by social circumstances, trade by barter succeeding seizure by raids, capital as labor saved for the future, and society’s four divisions — industrial, regulative, religious, and military — rising through fire, animals, slaves, and property.

“EMOTIONALLY, man transcends his animal ancestors in his ability to appreciate humor, art, and religion. Socially, man exhibits his superiority in that he is a toolmaker, a communicator, and an institution builder.”

“All human institutions minister to some social need, past or present, notwithstanding that their overdevelopment unfailingly detracts from the worth-whileness of the individual in that personality is overshadowed and initiative is diminished. Man should control his institutions rather than permit himself to be dominated by these creations of advancing civilization.”

What this paper teaches

The paper sorts institutions into families: those of self-maintenance, growing out of food hunger and self-preservation — industry, property, war for gain, and society’s regulative machinery — and those of self-perpetuation, growing out of sex hunger, maternal instinct, and the tender emotions — the home, the school, family life, education, ethics, and religion. It does not flinch from the dark entries in the record: primitive man never hesitated to enslave his fellows, and primitive society, though virtually communal, was not communism. Its verdict on our own age is pointed — all human institutions are merely the accumulated mores of the past, conserved by taboos and dignified by religion, until traditions harden into conventions.

Why it matters

This paper hands institution builders their charter and their warning in the same breath. The charter: institutions are how a people insures itself against famine, violence, and forgetting — building them is sacred, ordinary work. The warning is in the quote above: overdeveloped institutions overshadow personality and diminish initiative, so man must control his institutions rather than be dominated by them. And the record refuses to sanitize slavery, naming it plainly as part of primitive society’s machinery. A community building its own schools, banks, and councils today stands in this long line — and is told to keep the creations serving the people, never the reverse.
