What happened at Jesus' baptism and during the forty days?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 136 — Baptism and the Forty Days · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 136 opens Jesus' public work at the height of interest in John's preaching, when Palestine was aflame with expectancy. The paper draws the contrast plainly: John was an eager, earnest worker preaching repentance and flight from wrath; Jesus was a calm and happy laborer whose repentance was always followed by the gospel — the good tidings of the joy and liberty of the new kingdom. It then follows Jesus to baptism at the Jordan and up into the hills for the forty days of decision.

“When John preached the coming kingdom, the burden of his message was: Repent! flee from the wrath to come. When Jesus began to preach, there remained the exhortation to repentance, but such a message was always followed by the gospel, the good tidings of the joy and liberty of the new kingdom.”

What this paper teaches

The paper is mostly a record of decisions. It first surveys the competing concepts of the expected Messiah — deliverer, suffering servant, prophet-priest-king. At the Jordan, Jesus was baptized as Palestine engaged in solemn self-examination, and the voice of the Personalized Adjuster was heard speaking for the Universal Father. Then, up in the hills, Jesus settled his policies one by one: toward all classes of personalities, toward food and the care of his body, toward the use of superhuman power, and finally toward the methods by which the kingdom would be proclaimed. Only then did he come down the mountain.

Why it matters

Before Jesus preached a sermon or chose an apostle, he decided — alone, in the hills — what he would not do. No superhuman shortcuts, no spectacle to compel belief: the methods were settled before the mission began. That order matters for any movement. Power is most dangerous at the founding moment, when a leader can still choose the terms. Jesus' forty days say the terms are chosen in private, on purpose, before the crowd arrives. And the message itself was decided there too: repentance, yes — but always followed by joy and liberty, never ending in wrath.
