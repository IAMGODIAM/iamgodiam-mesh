How did the feeding of the five thousand set up the Capernaum crisis?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 152 — Events Leading up to the Capernaum Crisis · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 152 traces the swell of popularity that made the Capernaum crisis unavoidable. Jairus, a ruler of the synagogue, begs Jesus to heal his dying daughter, and crowds of the sick wait everywhere he goes. After days of teaching by the lake, more than five thousand people gather south of Bethsaida-Julias. Refusing to send them away hungry, Jesus feeds them from a lad's five barley loaves and two dried fishes.

“Before the apostles had an opportunity to express themselves, Jesus turned to Andrew and Philip, saying: “I do not want to send these people away. Here they are, like sheep without a shepherd. I would like to feed them. What food have we with us?” While Philip was conversing with Matthew and Judas, Andrew sought out the Mark lad to ascertain how much was left of their store of provisions. He returned to Jesus, saying: “The lad has left only five barley loaves and two dried fishes”—and Peter promptly added, “We have yet to eat this evening.”

What this paper teaches

The paper begins with the story of the cure of Amos already spread, a great crowd waiting, and the new observers from the Jerusalem Sanhedrin come to find cause against Jesus. Jairus hurries him toward his home, terribly impatient of the delay, while the sick press in along the way. Jesus goes on teaching by day and instructing the apostles and evangelists by night; when he withdraws across the lake, the people hire boats or walk around the upper end of the shore to find him. The multitude grows past a thousand, then three thousand, then about five thousand. After the feeding, the apostles are sent off alone by boat, and later Jesus holds informal afternoon conferences with the twelve before starting for Jerusalem on April 3.

Why it matters

The feeding of the five thousand begins in compassion, not spectacle: like sheep without a shepherd is how Jesus sees the crowd, and his first instinct is to feed them before sending them anywhere. For communities that have known scarcity, the scene dignifies both the hunger and the little that is brought — a lad's five barley loaves and two dried fishes, taken up and organized into companies of one hundred with a leader over each group. Abundance here is not magic detached from order; the paper itself calls it human pity plus creative power, shared in a fellowship where nobody is sent away hungry.
