What happened in Gethsemane on the night before the crucifixion?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 182 — In Gethsemane · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

About ten o'clock on Thursday night, Jesus led the eleven from the home of Elijah and Mary Mark toward the Gethsemane camp, with young John Mark following close behind. There Jesus asked his friends to draw apart with him and pray to the Father for strength to sustain them in that hour and in all the work ahead. He prayed at length for his followers, asking for unity among them but not uniformity. While he prayed and afterward, the apostles discovered that Judas was missing—and the discovery opened their eyes to his betrayal.

“A few moments after arriving at camp, Jesus said to them: “My friends and brethren, my time with you is now very short, and I desire that we draw apart by ourselves while we pray to our Father in heaven for strength to sustain us in this hour and henceforth in all the work we must do in his name.””

“Jesus prayed for unity among his followers, but he did not desire uniformity. Sin creates a dead level of evil inertia, but righteousness nourishes the creative spirit of individual experience in the living realities of eternal truth and in the progressive communion of the divine spirits of the Father and the Son. In the spiritual fellowship of the believer-son with the divine Father there can never be doctrinal finality and sectarian superiority of group consciousness.”

What this paper teaches

The paper moves through four scenes. First, the night walk to the camp, watched over by John Mark, who would witness everything from this night to the crucifixion. Second, the last prayer: Jesus kneeling in a circle with the eleven in full view of Jerusalem, praying for unity among his followers while insisting that righteousness nourishes individual experience rather than doctrinal finality. Third, the return to camp and the shock of finding Judas absent, when the Master's earlier words revealed that he had forsaken them. Finally, Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a nearby ravine, a place of earlier prayer, to commune with the Father.

Why it matters

Gethsemane shows a community praying on the edge of state violence, not escaping it. Jesus asks for strength to sustain the work, and his great prayer refuses the false choice between unity and freedom: sin levels people into inertia, but righteousness feeds the creative spirit of individual experience. A liberation movement can hear itself in that distinction—solidarity without sameness, fellowship without doctrinal fences. The night also names betrayal plainly. Judas is not explained away; his absence is faced in the circle he abandoned. Honest prayer, this paper suggests, makes room for both grief and resolve before the soldiers come.
