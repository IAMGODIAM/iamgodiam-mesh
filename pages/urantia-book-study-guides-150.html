What this paper teaches

On Sunday evening, January 16, Abner arrived with John's apostles for a joint conference with Jesus' group, taking up matters such as anointing the sick. Two days later the twenty-four were joined by about seventy-five tested evangelists; the evangelists went out in groups of five while Jesus and the twelve traveled together, the apostles going two and two. The paper calls commissioning ten women for gospel work Jesus' most amazing daring act: they were authorized to organize themselves, with funds and equipment provided through Judas, Andrew placed the Sabbath services in their hands, and Mary Magdalene became their most effective teacher. After an evening at Shunem, the party reached Nazareth, where Jesus walked unrecognized on Friday and faced friends and foes in the synagogue on the Sabbath.

Why it matters

The difference is who gets commissioned. Women are not edged into the work as helpers of the real ministers; they are set apart, authorized to organize themselves, funded from the common treasury, and entrusted with the Sabbath services — and the text names Mary Magdalene their most effective teacher. For communities whose women have always carried the faith while being denied the title, this paper reads like a restoration of the record. The tour also unites the two apostolic camps in one conference — John's men and Jesus' men planning together — before it ends in Nazareth, where the hometown crowd turns hostile. Authorization at Bethsaida, rejection at home: the paper holds both without flinching.
