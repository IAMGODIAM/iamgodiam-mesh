What happened in the four eventful days at Capernaum?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 145 — Four Eventful Days at Capernaum · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 145 opens the first open preaching tour of Galilee. With John the Baptist dead, Jesus returns to Capernaum in mid-January, instructs his apostles for three days at the Zebedee home, teaches by the seaside to crowds pressed to the water's edge, and preaches in the synagogue on the will of the Father in heaven. The Sabbath closes with an outburst of healing that sets all Capernaum talking — and leaves Jesus nearly sleepless while the apostles ride a wave of enthusiasm.

“JESUS and the apostles arrived in Capernaum the evening of Tuesday, January 13. As usual, they made their headquarters at the home of Zebedee in Bethsaida. Now that John the Baptist had been sent to his death, Jesus prepared to launch out in the first open and public preaching tour of Galilee. The news that Jesus had returned rapidly spread throughout the city, and early the next day, Mary the mother of Jesus hastened away, going over to Nazareth to visit her son Joseph.”

What this paper teaches

Arriving Tuesday evening, January 13, the party made headquarters at Zebedee's home in Bethsaida, spending Wednesday through Friday instructing the apostles and receiving earnest inquirers. On Friday the seaside crowd pressed him so near the water that fishermen rowed to his aid, and the celebrated draught of fishes is explained plainly: Jesus, an experienced fisherman, simply directed the men to where the fish were usually found. On the Sabbath, Peter preached on the kingdom in the morning, Andrew taught on the new way, and Jesus preached on the will of the Father in heaven, pressing the truth that religion is a personal experience. The day ended in a great outburst of healing, a rejoicing throng overrunning Zebedee's home, and a night when Jesus slept little.

Why it matters

This paper protects wonder from exploitation. When a catch of fish looks miraculous, the text itself gives the natural explanation — a teacher who knew the lake — rather than letting the crowd's hunger for signs do the talking. And its Sabbath sermon relocates religion from spectacle to the heart: a personal experience, an affair of the individual soul with God. For people long treated as an audience for other men's power, that is a liberation in itself. Notice, too, the quiet cost at the end: while the apostles glow with enthusiasm, the one who served the crowds lies awake. The paper honors unnoticed labor — the night work behind every public morning.
