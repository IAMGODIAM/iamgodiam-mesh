“WHEN Adam elected to leave the first garden to the Nodites unopposed, he and his followers could not go west, for the Edenites had no boats suitable for such a marine adventure. They could not go north; the northern Nodites were already on the march toward Eden. They feared to go south; the hills of that region were infested with hostile tribes. The only way open was to the east, and so they journeyed eastward toward the then pleasant regions between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. And many of those who were left behind later journeyed eastward to join the Adamites in their new valley home.”

What this paper teaches

The paper follows the rebuilding. The caravan took almost a full year to reach the Euphrates and waited out the flood before crossing to the land between the rivers. There Adam's people built new homes and a new center of culture and religion. Less than two years after Cain's birth, Abel was born — the first child born in the second garden. As time passed, the consequences of default grew plainer, yet Adam and Eve's repentance was accepted, and they died with strong faith in the promise that they would awake on the mansion worlds.

Why it matters

The second garden is the scripture of starting over. The first Eden was prepared for its tenants; the second had to be created by the labor of their own hands, in the sweat of their faces, on ground that felt cursed. Exile did not mean abandonment — repentance was accepted, and the story ends in faith, not disgrace. For a people whose history is a chain of forced removals and fresh starts, this paper dignifies the rebuild: what is made by hand after loss is no lesser garden, and the promise travels with the displaced.
