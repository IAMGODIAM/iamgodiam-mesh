What happened when life moved onto the land?

The era of exclusive marine life has ended. Paper 60 tells how land elevation, a cooling crust and cooling oceans, restricted and deepening seas, and a great increase of land in the northern latitudes changed the world's climate and opened the early land-life era — the age when the ancestors of the land vertebrates, heirs of the age of frogs, struggled through a hostile period and life gained its first foothold on dry ground.

What this paper teaches

The paper reads the rocks: the erosion deposits of this period were mostly conglomerates, shale, and sandstone, and the gypsum and red layers across America and Europe testify to an arid climate scourged by violent cloudbursts — in many regions a thousand feet of red sandstone holds no fossils at all, and land-animal life was continuous only in parts of Africa. About 150,000,000 years ago the early land-life periods begin; a new phase of the reptilian age opens 120,000,000 years ago. The great Cretaceous period takes its name from the prolific chalk-making foraminifers of the seas, and its close ends the great sea invasions of the continents.

Why it matters

Life's move onto land was not a coronation; it was a survival through scarcity. The frogs' descendants were no longer dominant, few types outlived the tribulation, and even the spore-bearing plants were nearly extinct — yet life did not fare so badly that it ended. It held on, in the places where holding on was possible, and in this paper one of those places is named: Africa, where the life of land animals was continuous when it vanished elsewhere. The continent so often written off in human histories appears here at the root of the story — the refuge where the line survived.
