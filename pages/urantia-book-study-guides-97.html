How did the Hebrew concept of God mature?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 97 — Evolution of the God Concept Among the Hebrews · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 97 follows the Hebrew God concept across a thousand years of teachers, kings, and prophets. Its opening marvel is that Hebrew leaders lifted Yahweh out of human likeness without turning him into a cold abstraction: even common people could regard him as a Father. From Samuel's reforms through the divided kingdoms, the clash with Baal, and Jeremiah's bold internationalizing of Yahweh, the paper traces a tribal god becoming a God who judges even his own people's crimes — and who lifts the poor.

“THE spiritual leaders of the Hebrews did what no others before them had ever succeeded in doing—they deanthropomorphized their God concept without converting it into an abstraction of Deity comprehensible only to philosophers. Even common people were able to regard the matured concept of Yahweh as a Father, if not of the individual, at least of the race.”

“Theretofore the Hebrews had regarded the favor of Yahweh mainly in terms of material prosperity. It was a great shock to Israel, and almost cost Samuel his life, when he dared to proclaim: “The Lord enriches and impoverishes; he debases and exalts. He raises the poor out of the dust and lifts up the beggars to set them among princes to make them inherit the throne of glory.” Not since Moses had such comforting promises for the humble and the less fortunate been proclaimed, and thousands of despairing among the poor began to take hope that they could improve their spiritual status.”

What this paper teaches

The paper moves era by era. Samuel, the rough-and-ready reformer, preached God's covenant-keeping sincerity and shocked Israel by proclaiming a Lord who raises the poor from the dust — though his Yahweh remained chiefly a tribal god. The narrative continues through the tenth-century division into two kingdoms, the long controversy with Baal — a socioeconomic clash of ideologies, the paper says — and the steps by which Yahweh became a God who punishes crime and immorality among his own people. Jeremiah takes the next bold step, internationalizing Yahweh; then come the captivity, the priesthood's resistance, the confusion of sacred and profane history, and the closing correction that “chosen” means chosen for service to every nation.

Why it matters

Samuel's proclamation is the heartbeat of this paper for any people who have been poor: “He raises the poor out of the dust and lifts up the beggars to set them among princes.” Not since Moses, the paper notes, had such promises been spoken to the humble — and despairing thousands took hope. The paper also refuses to romanticize the story: the fight with Baal was economics as much as theology, and the priesthood actively resisted the God concept's growth. Faith matures, this history teaches, when it stops flattering the powerful and starts answering to justice for everyone.
