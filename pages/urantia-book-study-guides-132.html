What did Jesus do during his sojourn at Rome?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 132 — The Sojourn at Rome · Part IV — The Life and Teachings of Jesus · reviewed 2026-10-16

Paper 132 follows Jesus in Rome, traveling as the scribe of Damascus with Gonod and Ganid, who carried greetings from the princes of India to the emperor Tiberius. Jesus had consented to the journey chiefly to study and mingle with the Empire's cosmopolitan peoples — southern Europe, Asia Minor, Syria, Egypt, and northwest Africa all gathered in one city. There, teaching mostly one person at a time, he quietly prepared thirty-two religious leaders, thirty of whom became pivotal in establishing Christianity in Rome.

“SINCE Gonod carried greetings from the princes of India to Tiberius, the Roman ruler, on the third day after their arrival in Rome the two Indians and Jesus appeared before him. The morose emperor was unusually cheerful on this day and chatted long with the trio. And when they had gone from his presence, the emperor, referring to Jesus, remarked to the aide standing on his right, “If I had that fellow’s kingly bearing and gracious manner, I would be a real emperor, eh?”

What this paper teaches

Most of the paper is conversations. Jesus held an all-night talk with Angamon, leader of the Stoics, teaching that true values are found on divine levels of eternal reality, not in material standards. Mardus, leader of Rome's Cynics, became his great friend. Nabon, a Greek Jew who led the chief Mithraic cult, also sat with him — and the paper notes that some of these contacts later helped turn the chief Mithraic temple into Rome's first Christian church. Jesus also spent hours returning a lost child to his anxious mother, met a rich Roman Stoic, and made five trips to points of interest around the city.

Why it matters

Rome was the capital of an empire built on conquest, and Jesus did his work there without office, army, or announcement — one conversation at a time, often with a single listener. The paper stresses that these men and women were not tradition bound, and that none of them even knew who had taught them. For communities that have had to build under empires, that is a recognizable pattern: transformation travels by personal contact, below the notice of power, and its fruit appears a generation later. The emperor on his throne noticed only a kingly bearing; the real founding happened in side rooms.
