How did the institution of marriage develop?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 83 — The Marriage Institution · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Marriage began, this paper recounts, in the loose and promiscuous matings of the herd, and progressed through many variations and adaptations until it arrived at pair mating — one man and one woman establishing a home of the highest social order. Marriage is society's mechanism for regulating the human relations that arise from bisexuality, and what forever safeguards it is simple: men and women positively will not live without each other.

“THIS is the recital of the early beginnings of the institution of marriage. It has progressed steadily from the loose and promiscuous matings of the herd through many variations and adaptations, even to the appearance of those marriage standards which eventually culminated in the realization of pair matings, the union of one man and one woman to establish a home of the highest social order.”

“The family, which grows out of marriage, is itself a stabilizer of the marriage institution together with the property mores. Other potent factors in marriage stability are pride, vanity, chivalry, duty, and religious convictions. But while marriages may be approved or disapproved on high, they are hardly made in heaven. The human family is a distinctly human institution, an evolutionary development. Marriage is an institution of society, not a department of the church. True, religion should mightily influence it but should not undertake exclusively to control and regulate it.”

What this paper teaches

The paper walks through marriage's early forms: primitive marriages planned by parents; the ancients' mistrust of love and promises, which demanded tangible security in property; the wedding ceremony's origin in marriage as a community affair rather than a private decision. It describes the era when unmarried women belonged to the men of the tribe, weighs monogamy as a monopoly that blesses those who attain it while working hardship on others, and recalls the loose early unions in which children followed the mother. It closes by insisting that marriage culminating in the home is man's most exalted institution — but essentially human, never properly a sacrament.

Why it matters

There is a quiet dignity in how this paper tells the story: marriage was not handed down perfect from heaven but built, slowly and sometimes painfully, by ordinary people groping toward stable homes. Communities that were denied legal marriage for generations — whose unions existed without license or protection — were not outside this institution; they were living its oldest truth, that the bond rests on the people's own faithfulness, not on paper. And the insistence that marriage is human, not a sacrament, returns it to the couples and communities who actually sustain it.
