Where did worship begin?

IAMGODIAM Knowledge Mesh · Urantia Book Study Guides · Paper 85 — The Origins of Worship · Part III — The History of Urantia · reviewed 2026-10-16

Worship began, this paper teaches, with fear. Primitive religion had a biologic origin, a natural evolutionary development apart from moral associations and spiritual influences: the higher animals have fears but no illusions, while man creates his primitive religions out of his fears and by means of his illusions. The first object worshiped was a stone; from stones and plants and animals, worship climbed to the elements and finally to the sun, moon, and stars.

“At one time or another mortal man has worshiped everything on the face of the earth, including himself. He has also worshiped about everything imaginable in the sky and beneath the surface of the earth. Primitive man feared all manifestations of power; he worshiped every natural phenomenon he could not comprehend. The observation of powerful natural forces, such as storms, floods, earthquakes, landslides, volcanoes, fire, heat, and cold, greatly impressed the expanding mind of man. The inexplicable things of life are still termed “acts of God” and “mysterious dispensations of Providence.””

What this paper teaches

The paper catalogues the first objects of human worship: stones, venerated and even anointed, as with Jacob's stone; plants, feared then worshiped because of intoxicating liquors; the higher animals, toward which primitive man felt a peculiar fellow feeling; and earth, air, water, and fire. It shows how veneration of rocks, hills, trees, and animals developed into deification of the sun, moon, and stars, how nature worship envisioned nature spirits for lakes, waterfalls, and rain, and how man, having worshiped everything else, did not hesitate to worship himself. Through it all, the sixth adjutant spirit was quietly at work in primitive minds.

Why it matters

This paper honors the ancestors instead of mocking them. The grandmother who feared storms, the elder who kept a sacred stone — they were not fools; they were minds doing serious work with the experience they had, and even their illusions carried the seed of true worship. For communities whose indigenous and African spiritual practices were condemned as superstition by the very people who stole them, this account matters: it treats early worship everywhere as one human story, dignified in its beginnings and still unfolding toward the light. Reverence did not begin in temples built by empires; it began wherever frightened, hopeful people looked up.
